the social reckoning, 1644-1900

Oct 18, 2026

One of the general principles through which I approach understanding the current period we are living in (and, indeed, a principle which has yet to prove me wrong) is that we are actually living in a profoundly normal period in human history and our concerns are about as unprecedented as and complex as those of any normal period in human history, insofar the particular amalgamation of things that comprises our current societal problems is essentially as unique as that of any time period: just as the particular set of events that led to the particular assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand can effectively be absorbed into the broader systemic trends of the time which had been whispering ‘mass unrest in Europe’ for quite a while (and not even necessarily in the form of a war, though that was how it eventually manifested), so can our own present situation be understood in terms of the same, complex societal factors that have historical precedent, even if not in the specifics. Of course, anomalies do happen—Alexander happened, and then Caesar (both of them!), then Genghis Khan, and then Napoleon, and so on, and we may yet be on the brink of another historically anomalous situation with respect to the specifics of our overlords, but broadly speaking the manner of this ‘grand societal correction’ that occurs may be anything, but that doesn’t mean it won’t occur nevertheless.

Take technology. Everyone around you will tell you that the march of technological progress is inevitable and attempting to utilize technology as a means to support societal progress rather than adapt societal progress to this inevitable march of technology is foolhardy. This has certainly been true for at least a few hundred years now! Technology becomes increasingly sophisticated, vast numbers of people die or are rendered unemployed, Ellul’s technique spreads its hand over the masses of human civilization, and civilization continues to spread forward and occasionally prosper in tandem with that technology. Of course, these sentiments are motivated not by empirical observation—which would of course reveal this simplicity to be false—but because we happen to be living in the global maxima of technological progress. There is little reason to assume that we have now hit an ever-increasing scale (because the universe and the resources in it are finite, there is by definition no ever-increasing scale, though one would hope that the peak of this particular global maxima arises far, far before we ever hit anything even concerning planetary scales, let alone universal ones) or that technological progress will not inevitably plateau before falling, headfirst, again. Indeed, this plateauing has quite a bit of precedent in human civilization. Here is, for instance, an (incomplete) list of technologies that were widespread within the Roman Empire and yet failed to remain commonplace in medieval Europe after its fall, going so far as to have been subsequently rediscovered even a thousand years after C.E. 5001: concrete, pottery, aqueducts, indoor plumbing, central heating, ceramic tiling, glassmaking, roads, coinage—the list goes on, and the Roman Empire isn’t even the most egregious example of a significant subsequent technological downturn. Take Mycenaean Greece, for instance, which lost writing for about four hundred years after Bronze Age Collapse, or the loss of pyramid-building technology leading to the thousand-year-old question of how did the Egyptians do it, the answers to which have included ‘aliens.’

Similarly, people try to make this argument about how technologies that exist eventually get used, but this is again historically incorrect: human cloning isn’t a thing, people banned CFCs, leaded gasoline, asbestos, laser weapons, etc. Just because they were widespread at some point doesn’t mean that people didn’t retrospectively realized they sucked big time and got rid of them, and it also doesn’t mean that several of the current technologies we take for granted won’t be retrospectively considered similarly barbaric, banned, and people three hundred years from now will wonder how we could possibly have been so stupid to use them so relentlessly.

Of course, just because our problems have historical precedent doesn’t mean that they are not problems, it’s just that this notion of ‘oh my God we are totally doomed forever and we’re going to be living in sci-fi dystopia until the end of time!!’ is a generally hysterical one that isn’t really rooted in any kind of stable framework that involves reality2. But one thing about having historical precedent is that it often lets us estimate how far along we are in terms of such corrections inevitably happening; furthermore, if our knowledge of modern science, anthropology and sociology tells us anything it is that one thing we have actually got a little better at recently is actually doing something to address the societal problems that we have (I wouldn’t say that we’re very good at this, but there have been societies which were quite a bit worse). For example, people recently have been worrying about an AI bubble, which almost certainly exists, and while it is true that as of recent AI-related stocks account for about 45% of the S&P 500, this itself isn’t an imminent sign of the bubble being close to popping3 because railway stocks comprised about 71% of the total UK market in 1848 which isn’t even in the same ballpark, really.

old man sorkin

All that preamble I didn’t even arrive at the second core thesis of my general line of reasoning, which is that all politics is essentially a materialist politics. This will make more sense in due course, once I make my way through why I’m thinking about this kind of technological determinism in the first place: it’s spurred, unfortunately, by a Friday-night viewing of Aaron Sorkin’s new film The Social Reckoning, which is supposed to be a spiritual sequel to his and Fincher’s 2011 masterpiece The Social Network. Unfortunately The Social Reckoning is much more of a literal sequel to The Social Network than a spiritual one. Fincher’s movie is really really good. Sorkin’s new film is pretty awful. And this isn’t even because of Mikey Madison and Jeremy Allen White (both of whom I find myself always determined to hate but ultimately do find pleasant on screen) but because Sorkin’s ‘sauce’ which has always been flavored with a little bit of a preachy and distracting utopian liberalism has finally been lost which means that the neoliberalism is all that remains. I always think back to the old Truffaut take of how there are no anti-war films because cinema glorifies whatever it displays by making it look pretty and heightened4 when I think of biopics—they, too, can’t help but glorify their subject—and how this new onslaught of tech biopics we’re getting these days (Zuckerberg, Altman, Elizabeth Holmes, SBF, ugh) are going to deal with the problem of accidentally rehabilitating the most reprehensible drug-addled lizards on Earth by making them look actually interesting through giving them a highlight in the form of movie instead of like the mind-numbingly boring lizards that they are in real life5. Sorkin’s approach is to make Zuck look and feel like a Bond villain while Bill Burr screams at him. There’s also this scene where Jan 6th is going on and everyone’s looking at Zuck asking him to personally delete the Facebook livestream in the ‘war room’ which openly advocates for the fact that Facebook should basically regulate the content on their platform, a really beautiful can of worms only Aaron Sorkin could serve to you on a platter because of Newsroom-era insistence that the institutions of American politics are wonderful, it’s just the people in them who suck, as if it weren’t the case that the institutions—inbuilt with a complex system of checks and balances—were successively weakened over the years in a deliberate attempt to reduce corporate regulation and consolidate executive power6.

Anyway, this movie sucks and is saved to some extent by the fact that Mikey Madison is both pretty and pretty great in it, and also because it’s generally fun to watch. Setting aside Sorkin’s stupid insistence that Facebook needs to start regulating the content on its platform7, however, is the more important question of what is to be done. Here is a technology that has intensely negative impacts on both mental health and the health of political discourse in a country, is essentially one of the most successful mass surveillance mechanisms of all time, algorithmically radicalizes the masses into consumerism and political negligence, somehow has also been linked with declining birth rates, of all things, makes people stupider and destroys their attention spans, carries with it all the negative consequences of television that better writers like DFW or Baudrillard have pointed out already ad nauseam, and also, as per Sam Kriss, serves as a refuge for demons:

The internet is not a communications system. Instead of delivering messages between people, it simulates the experience of being among people, in a way that books or shopping lists or even the telephone do not. And there are things that a simulation will always fail to capture. In the philosophy of Emmanuel Lévinas, your ethical responsibility to other people emerges out of their face, the experience of looking directly into the face of another living subject. “The face is what prohibits us from killing.” Elsewhere: “The human face is the conduit for the word of God.” But Facebook is a world without faces. Only images of faces; selfies, avatars: dead things. Or the moving image in a FaceTime chat: a haunted puppet. There is always something in the way. You are not talking to a person: the machine is talking, through you, to itself.

Provocative and essentially correct, but what I am drawn to is this later section from his sequel article The Internet is Already Over:

In the future—not the distant future, but ten years, five—people will remember the internet as a brief dumb enthusiasm, like phrenology or the dirigible. They might still use computer networks to send an email or manage their bank accounts, but those networks will not be where culture or politics happens. The idea of spending all day online will seem as ridiculous as sitting down in front of a nice fire to read the phone book. Soon, people will find it incredible that for several decades all our art was obsessed with digital computers: all those novels and films and exhibitions about tin cans that make beeping noises, handy if you need to multiply two big numbers together, but so lifeless, so sexless, so grey synthetic glassy bugeyed spreadsheet plastic drab. And all your smug chortling over the people who failed to predict our internetty present—if anyone remembers it, it’ll be with exactly the same laugh.

Kriss wrote this in 2022. Five years is perhaps too eerily prescient to be true, but the ten years is looking like an inevitability at this point: and it comes out of not some kind of public push towards increasing mass morality, but through the eventual transformation of the internet into the kind of ‘agentic wasteland’ which renders it essentially obsolete. Ultimately, the facade of social media is that humans are interacting with humans; interacting with non-humans via such mechanisms carries no such appeal. Bots have already rid myself of my own social media existence, which remains limited to, say, 20 mins a day of Instagram (although if you count messaging apps as social media then that’s a different thing altogether—I do spend a lot of time talking to real people over the internet) and recent grumbles from my parents about LLM-generated content flooding the mainstream and causing misunderstandings to outright cyberattacks seems to point towards a promising endpoint of the internet being essentially unusable in any social or even information-receiving capacity, with made-up news becoming more of an algorithmic frenzy than ever; people will soon only be able to pay attention to the things that happen locally, physically around them, because the only things that are real, of course—even while the internet was going on well and good—were those that happened in front of your own eyes.

Ultimately the distinction to me remains that AI and the internet are fundamentally the same thing: the embodiment of the emergent human consciousness, and while it used to be run by humans themselves they’ve now given a name and a face to it and seem to be selling it as super-intelligence8.

neoliberal accelerator

There is a scientific sense in which AI and the internet are not the same thing: informally speaking you can categorize new forms of technology into technologies of efficiency and technologies of power, and the differences in how they affect society stems from the ways they manifest as technologies of power.

Take the mass spectrometer. This is a device that is used to measure the mass-to-charge ratio of particles. The mass spectrometer is a staple instrument of chemists because it fundamentally does something that human beings cannot do: no amount of human intellectual capability or effort can result in the kind of precision that a mass spectrometer provides, and in doing so it provides humans a qualitatively new way of doing a particular thing—in this case, measuring the mass-to-charge ratio of particles. The nuclear bomb is also such a technology of power, because it enables the kind of mass destruction that is otherwise impossible for a human being to accomplish. So is the lightbulb, or the microwave, or the refrigerator, or contraceptives, or pasteurization. These technologies enable human beings to do something that they qualitatively were unable to do before. They fundamentally create something new.

This is not what the internet is, or what the car is. These are primarily technologies of efficiency: they allow humans to do something in a much faster and more efficient manner—communication and transportation, namely. LLMs, similarly, are technologies of acceleration: fundamentally they mimic human cognitive behavior, they just do it at scale and very quickly. Because of the efficiency with which these things process collective human tasks they make it possible to monetize certain metrics of human social behavior that otherwise remains natural. For example, I can always talk to my friend for free, but if I want to do it right now and from anywhere on earth and as fast as possible you can charge me a fee to do that. Of course, the resulting transaction is merely a simulation of the social desire I possessed—in reality I am not talking to my friend over the telephone, I am merely communicating with the actual phone itself, which is repeating the words my friend says. What the telephone does in this sense is decouple the act of socialization with the act of communication. To talk to my friend I ordinarily need to perform the necessary social rituals; shake his hand, perhaps, or dress to some inordinate degree—I need to do none of these things over the phone, and so the manner of communication is different. Talking over the phone is a conversation consisting of merely relaying information to each other—it does not take advantage of the shared social and physical environment you both inhabit, or your surroundings, or the natural friction of co-inhabited spaces. In some sense, it is a lot more like quickly scribbling letters to each other—which is, in fact, what texting is.

This decoupling of social rituals with the actual phenomenon of communication has interesting effects. For example, it is well-known that people are more likely to be flaky when they have the option of quick communication—it is a lot easier to cancel on someone than to not turn up and suffer the social consequences of making them wait, which is a significant inconvenience on your part. Socially, the internet and LLMs push this a step further: they decouple the already-weakened socialization of telephone calls with mere simulation of conversation, eliminating any of the social friction required to keep even a telephone call sustainable (the requirement of saying “Hello,” for instance, or the implicit knowledge that the person you are talking to you have a relationship with outside the telephone and therefore could potentially suffer consequences for when it comes to the violation of a social contract). In this way it is possible to pepper over the natural consequences of human interaction that lead to reductions in productivity and instead achieve a wholehearted society of efficiency through the use of social engineering technologies.

People didn’t have anyone to talk to at home, so they used to know their neighbors: now you can talk to whoever you want, so there’s no need to!

Of course, the meaningful hit here is terms of the quality of life of the actual human beings involved, but neoliberal thought considers humans to be secondary to the optimization activity of production and capital accumulation. In this sense, LLMs when viewed as social or efficiency technologies are essentially technologies that decouple the human activities of scientific thought with the accumulation of scientific results, because scientific results are gameable: you can make money off harnessing lightning, but you can’t make money off the intellectual phenomenon of the enlightenment which was equally guided by scientific thought! This phenomenon of neoliberal acceleration essentially seeks to eliminate all human intellectual and spiritual achievement and turn humanity into something Borg-like, concerned primarily with efficiency or technique above all costs.

This is all well and good, if not for several basic scientific observations that render the neoliberal dream dead in the water:

  1. Humans are sustainable. Essentially all the resources required to maintain human civilization at a base scale are renewable. You can grow food and trees. You cannot produce cobalt out of nowhere—once it’s run out, it’s run out, and then you can’t make batteries anymore.
  2. Humans have an extraordinary amount of collective power. If you render the masses extremely unhappy, they will kill you.
  3. The cognitive rewiring that social engineering technologies do make them effectively unsustainable. Social media derangement leads to bizarre and incoherent political ideologies that fracture the whole concept of politics in the first place. LLM use makes people stupid and incapable at performing tasks without LLMs, which renders them unable to fix problems caused by the aforementioned LLMs9.

Nevertheless, it is not true that these things cannot cause mass harm! The conclusion is that the neoliberal dream does not really have any kind of logical ending beyond an unsustainable equilibrium of feudalism10, and the question is exactly how far these new AI technologies take along that road.

And just as it took us 20 years to realize that the internet was essentially a bit of a crockpot, so do I think it will take us another 40, maybe 50 at most before all these things: social media hallucinations, LLM psychosis—these things go away. They will not be around forever, no matter what people tell you. This is the real world, not some kind of psychological playground. Either people learn to live sustainably with social media in a way that it actively enhances the quality of life of the citizens of the world—or at the very least the downside does not result in fertility decline so severe that the human race becomes incapable of propagating—or it will be banned. They will get rid of it.

It seems inconceivable. But, as every historical event has precedent, so does this one.

the chinese opioid crisis

In 1799 the Qing empire banned opium importation. Over the past 100 years, opium in China had grown from being a medicinal drug—used to cure diarrhea, mainly—to being used as a recreational narcotic. The number of users was in the millions. Government clerks, imperial eunuchs—no party was left free from the confines of opium, and recreational opium consumption was a practice considered characteristic of modernity and upper-class bourgeois culture. Nearly all of this opium was imported from Britain, from the East India Company which manufactured it in Bengal and licensed private traders to ship it to China11. By 1838, the amount of opium being privately imported to China was around 40,000 chests.

Opium, more than anything else, ended up being one of the primary catalysts for what the Chinese would later rechristen the ‘Century of Humiliation.’ By 1813, smoking opium was not only illegal, but punishable by law—100 lashes were guaranteed to anyone who would be caught smoking the product. Of course, opium was also vastly addictive, and to stop using it was to give oneself up to a vast number of physical withdrawal symptoms. In the words of politician Lin Zexu— one of the primary proponents of the ‘punish pushers’ approach to opium legislation in the mid-1800s—writing to Queen Victoria:

Formerly, smokers were few in number; but of late the contagion has spread, and its flowing poison has daily increased […] But ever since the great Ch’ing dynasty united the empire, its aim has been to regulate manners and customs with the view of rectifying the heart of man; how then can our House allow those who live within the girdle of the Seas to poison themselves at their own sweet will? Therefore, all who trade in or smoke opium in the Inner Land will be most severely punished, and the introduction and circulation of the drug will be for ever prohibited. It appears that this particular form of poison is illegally prepared by scoundrels in the tributary tribes of your honourable country and in the devil-regions under your jurisdiction; but of course it is neither prepared nor sold by your sovereign orders. Further, that it is not all nations but only some which prepare this article; and that you do not allow your own people to smoke, under severe penalties for disobedience, evidently knowing what a curse it is and therefore strictly prohibiting the practice. But better still than forbidding people to smoke, would it not be to forbid the sale and also the preparation of opium?

The opium situation in China eventually got so bad that an enormous amount of Chinese silver was being exchanged for opium, leading the kingdom to financial ruin—and by 1840 British commercial interests had grown so strong that they could only be managed by war. 20 years of alternating war and unequal ceasefire was followed by an 1860 regulation legalizing opium. By 1906, 25% of China’s male population was addicted to opium. Finally, legislation in 1906 began the long process of reversing the opium crisis, a process which was only brought to total completion with the reforms of Mao Zedong starting from 1849.

The whole process took 150 years.

The opium crisis remains one of the bedrocks of Chinese history and the national consciousness. The loss in the first Opium War against the British Empire destroyed the Chinese international position. It was one of the fundamental policy proposals that led to a turning point in the history of the Qing Empire, and the devastating effects of the opium mania are well-found throughout the annals of Chinese history.

In contrast, the social media arguably age started in 2007, with the launch of the iPhone. These ‘disruptive’ technologies proved not only to be the stalwarts of the history of global capitalism, but also enormously consequential to the generations that were raised on them—and despite warnings from as far back as 2008 that the infinite scroll would lead to mass losses in attention span and critical thinking abilities no attempts were made rid them of their addictive potential or of the effect they had on the human consciousness. All the prophecies of the social media detractors are now true: the average rates of reading and writing comprehension, along with IQ—flawed a measure as it is—are decreasing; the President is elected based on tiktok palatability rather than any kind of economic platform, unemployment continues to rise, the consolidation of the sources of production has never been more naked than before, and algorithmic advances and recommendation systems have led to widespread failure of the 20th century cultural systems—like always, capitalism is eating its own customers, having failed to see them as anything but units of production.

Of course, there have not been any Opium Wars, either, so it’s very possible that the ‘century of humiliation’ has, in fact, not yet begun—perhaps LLMs will prove to be the disrupter that ends the first ‘cycle’ of the internet, but hardly can anyone call them a superior replacement. It took 100 years after the Opium Wars for the crisis to end. It got worse before it got better. There will, inevitably, be a day that humans are free from the handcuffs of these technologies that seek to do little but sap away our freedom, free once again on the edge of the American frontier—but there may be a century-wide gulf of dystopia until that day arrives and we must ensure until that time that the lessons of the 21st century will not be long-forgotten, take all the opportunities we get to denounce and decry these oppressive tools that seek only to alienate us from ourselves, to dehumanize people and turn them into slovenly beasts of their own nature. If history tells us anything, it is that while the day of reckoning does eventually arrive, there is no limit to how bad things can get in the interim.
