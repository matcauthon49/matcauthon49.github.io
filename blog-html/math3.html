doctor lazarus

Nov 27, 2026

Now that the LLM revolution is finally upon us there’s a variety of conversation about what mathematics actually is and how the machines don’t really do mathematics because instead of simply proving theorems mathematics also involves understanding theorems and synthesizing their techniques in order to come up with new ideas and questions, not to mention consolidating this knowledge and transmitting it to the newer generation. There is merit to this thought, but it ultimately does contain this large element of contending with the incoming catastrophe by radically redefining mathematical work to something that it has essentially never been, not really. It is true that math is more than just proving theorems, but it’s true insofar as football is just scoring touchdowns—there’s a lot more to it than that, but ultimately the core activity around which the whole shebang is organized is proving theorems, and now that that’s been taken away from mathematicians it begs the question of what the point of the activity really is. Without touchdowns you’re just throwing the ball around and hitting each other—it’s fun to do, sure, but it’s not really any kind of sport you can organize something around. If you can’t score touchdowns, you can’t have the super bowl, either, and what’s football without the super bowl?

I’m not saying I like this new set of affairs, but part of the reason this whole set of affairs has even come about is because there’s a sense in which the mathematical activity we used to do until very recently was not entirely healthy, either. I mean, it’s not just AI which has popped up and upended our profession; there’s also this formal verification business which puts a pretty poor taste in my mouth, on the whole, and is also comically well-timed with the AI catastrophe, not just in how it manages to ‘solve’ the various problems associated with AI slop—here’s an unambiguous way to check whether the AI slop actually is slop or not, an activity that would otherwise take mathematicians years to do but would also allow them to process the mathematical reasoning underlying these slop proofs and, perhaps, in the process, allow them to actually do mathematics instead of razing it! Very convenient, though it is true that the development of these things has been heavily linked—the idea of doing an AlphaGo over formal logic to find proofs is a pretty old one, culminating in what I think is quite handily one of the grossest things the mathematical community has done in its several hundred years of organized existence—allowed these clowns to attempt the formal verification and subsequent automation of a high-school math olympiad, which had already in turn grossly been transformed from an event constructed to facilitate a gathering of talented youngsters into a factory farm for talent mining. I’m willing to bet that of all the ‘youth events’ in the world the IMO is probably the one with the most dystopian and sinister span of sponsors. Just look at that beautiful list.

Why is it that a bunch of mathematicians over the last couple decades suddenly became a fan of this new mathematical ‘compiler’ that takes in your argument and checks whether it is true? The obvious motivation seems to be that once you can transform the heavy abstractness of math into formally verifiable reasoning you can play all sorts of games with it like performing alpha-beta pruning and developing synthetic proofs of your arguments, so the ultimate goal was always that you could basically get a means to check whether any statement that you could encode in formal logic was in some sense ‘true.’ But this is a goal much older than Kevin Buzzard et al—it’s a goal that goes as far back as Hilbert and Gödel or even Bertrand Russell & co. who sought the launch the first real formal experimentation into whether such a thing was even possible. This is the kind of thing that I have always found fascinating but only inasmuch as it was a thought experiment; the idea of actually having a machine to solve the Entscheidungsproblem is of course nightmarish in its implications. But the more, for lack of a better word, playful considerations of Gödel and Turing were ultimately key to opening a pretty rich set of ideas into the nature of computation and also fortunately answered the literal question in the negative, though maybe leaving the more morally correct question unanswered—is there an algorithm that can solve every interesting problem one can frame in the language of set theory? It seems like there is1.

Of course, Hilbert’s machine would not give a proof ‘from the book’—it would just give a binary output. The machine that we do have gives something that is a lot closer to a proof from the book. But then what a proof from the book is depends quite a bit more on you than on the book—is the proof of the Weil Conjectures a proof ‘from the book’? Our Bourbaki friends certainly thought so, though that’s the kind of reasoning that also leads you to say things like ‘actually this is trivially true by Yoneda’s lemma’ and things like that and consider that somehow satisfactory. I’ve always thought that Euclid was very good at proofs from the book; the one of the infinitude of the primes has always been my favorite, and the only other person I’d say who would get pretty close with some amount of frequency was Erdős; if there’s anything that’s in the book it’s got to be the probabilistic method. There are even those who’d say that the four-color theorem’s current proof is in the book, too, and those are the kind of people that you’d ideally want burned at stake, and a part of me suspects that those are exactly the kind of people who very much enjoy this beautiful new wave of LLM mathematics and slop proofs such as this new ETH disproof which has appeared in a pretty ugly, ungainly manner and (as the streets are saying) if it is indeed true that P = BQP it should be completely recharacterized2 in that context. But in any case we don’t really live in that hypothetical world and I doubt the one in which we had Hilbert’s machine would be a good or pretty one, either; that would be the world which would turn mathematics into something like a natural science, where we fan the landscape looking for things like ‘the tallest mountain’ (a polynomial-time algorithm for factorization) and then go on expeditions to get there (finding the actual algorithm that runs on your computer). That’s a romantic way of putting it, but even continuing that analogy it would turn Lewis and Clark into Alex Honnold, something that—fun as Honnold is—cannot result in anything but an eventual disenchantment. Are we not cowboys on the frontier? Without that impetus it would ultimately result in academic mathematics becoming even more of this game of difficult problem-solving rather than exploring the Sierra Nevada; now math is just the IMO, which I’m sure is perfectly palatable to guys like Buzzard, but quite indigestible to the rest of us.

The other reason for ‘lean’ is of course the age-old question of correctness, which pulls on metascientific literature that has concluded that something like 70% of math papers are incorrect and prove false theorems or something. I mean, I’m glad to give this to them, because it hardly matters whether most papers are correct—all that matters is whether all math papers of consequence are correct, and every time one of these bombs drops math is temporarily suspended as an activity in favor of subjecting the bomb to the utmost scrutiny and establishing its potentially radioactive effects to a p-value far beyond what even the most disingenuous of physicists would require. I have no doubt the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem is correct, and I haven’t even read it! If it weren’t, that would be a problem—but if the latest ‘proof’ of the twisted Zarankiewiscz problem for m = 5 is incorrect it hardly matters because nobody is reading it anyway. In fact from what I can tell lean is proving me correct; none of the big papers have mistakes in them (or if they are, they’re completely inconsequential and can be easily fixed), so that’s dead in the water. Therefore I can only be forced to conclude that lean is nothing but a support system for machine mathematics and should really only be seen as such. We have enough on our hands without requiring every new bit of mathematical insight to come stamped with a vibecoded seal of approval.

Famously, Terry Tao these days has taken to making pronouncements about how mathematics is being strip-mined, but unfortunately his own history with respect to these formalization efforts makes me not really take these objections coming from him seriously (correct as he may be)—what exactly did he think would happen when he allied himself with these monstrous forces of capital? I presume his mathematical utopia was one where he would continue to do the things he wanted and occasionally ask the God-machine for hints, who would give him just enough that he would feel both proud of solving the Riemann Hypothesis as well as maintain the fiction that it was him and not the machine which actually did it. Levent Alpöge, who in fact did solve the Riemann Hypothesis, has recently taken to singing that very same song. Obviously Levent knows (not even particularly deep down or anything, it’s quite straightforwardly true) that he did not solve the Riemann Hypothesis. In the grand scheme of things however this is all short-lived. The only reason that all this is really a matter of debate is because things only happen because humans are there to witness it, and in this case people are of course more interested in the question of whether Levent actually solved the problem or not because a machine proof is only of marginal interest to them. The chess analogy does get you this far: to other mathematicians math is indeed in many ways a spectator sport, and just as no one cared about how Gary Kasparov lost to Deep Blue and only that he lost to deep blue the only thing people care about now is whether Levent did prove Riemann or didn’t, and taking the analogy to its natural conclusion it’s only a short time before the people who actually do mathematics will only really do human mathematics. Who watches computer chess? Nobody roots for stockfish, they only root for the guys who designed the latest stockfish!

Unlike chess, however, which is a game, and you reset the board every time, math is a process of discovery and once ‘strip-mined’ doesn’t yield other things. But this is of course not what the powers that be think of math. The powers that be love lean; they think that producing incorrect papers is a big problem that must be immediately rectified because it would mean that their money is being used for frivolous ends. The powers that be also dislike the idea of people checking proofs at all—this is time that could be spent coming up with and proving new (correct) theorems. Since the powers that be are concerned largely with the unit economics of mathematical discovery rather than the intellectual process of it, to them more theorems = good. Such thinking of course immediately motivates them to ally themselves with machine cultists and pour in vast amounts of capital towards the ends of intellectual automation. That said, while they don’t really understand the cultural practice of mathematics they certainly have correctly identified the goals of the current mathematical infrastructure, which is really only concerned with producing more mathematics. Paradoxically, the way it is set up results in exactly the ends we have now, ie. paving the way for automation. The only reason people were trying to automate mathematics in the first place is because mathematics just wasn’t progressing very well. There were all these hypotheticals about math becoming too hard for individual humans and requiring too many years of training, despite the fact that in the past we were happily able to simplify things and enhance pedagogy despite such an argument being equally valid even back in the early 20th century. And we solved these problems in the 20th century not by entertaining ourselves through scaling GPUs but in the most human way possible: by accelerating mathematics through the grand development of beautiful mathematical cultures.

academia is kind of already dead

Of the many beautiful mathematical cultures we have created the current infrastructure of academia is not one of them. This is one of the least original statements I have ever written on this blog. The point about the misaligned incentives of academia has been repeated ad nauseam, and most of the observations—even sophisticated ones framed in terms of jargon like ‘Goodhart’s law’—are easily explained by the fact that human beings are products of ego and yearn to be respected, and so instead of ‘producing brilliant mathematical insights’ they do the thing that will get them the respect they desire, which is write as many papers as possible. Now why exactly people have coalesced into this particular ‘mode’ of research transmission is unclear (it probably has to do with historic reasons) but in general, distributed, ‘globalized’ anything has never really been a way to create intellectual value, and in recent years this has to have been accelerated by all this new kind of technological infrastructure we have created that of course attempts to do absolutely nothing but demolish human culture as we know it. I mean, I’m certain that Masullo’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is incorrect or at least incomplete, but the current academic infrastructure actively disincentivizes such lofty goals in the first place unless you’ve established yourself or whatever (though one can always see how Atiyah is perceived these days). But even more than that the way that the internet and social systems operate these days involves a fair amount of insincerity. It just feels stupid to believe in noble things when faced with masses of ironic disdain being heaped upon you (even if the disdain is mostly hypothetical—a lot of it operates around how one is ‘perceived’ to an imaginary audience). No authentic statement of self-values is really allowed to take hold. Take, for example, these two descriptions of an ‘authentic’ mathematical culture that produced extraordinary results. The first is me paraphrasing Göttingen, while the second is from Russell Impagliazzo’s memoir of Steven Rudich at Berkeley:

In that sense, the prime achievement of Göttingen was not the beautiful mathematics that they produced, but the shared generation of mathematicians. Foremost among this achievement was that Göttingen was a physical location. The university town was located far enough from Berlin that distractions were far and few between, and the layout of the university was structured around maximizing in-person interaction. Göttingen recruited indiscriminately, but also in a selfish manner—researchers were expected to reside in the city of Göttingen and partake in all the shared rituals of the community, which included the Lesezimmer, or the reading room, which formed a library from which books could not be taken out and which was easy to gain access to, and the Bonzenspaziergang, a multi-hour weekly meeting of professors (outdoors, usually) where they were forbidden to talk about research but rather would spend time discussing further planned seminars, courses and about their general life. Mathematics pervaded every aspect of the culture of Göttingen. Hilbert took daily walks with students discussing the ideas within his lectures; he encouraged them to read the classical textbooks, and he himself collaborated with quite a number of them on their own projects. Mathematics was not a career with a target goal of theorem production: it was a way of life in the strictest sense of the term. The great researchers of the day were not merely colleagues, they were friends, existing within an ecosystem that provided a number of characters with their own distinctive outlooks on the future of mathematics. And it functioned particularly well as a single organism that revolutionized the mathematical culture.

At Berkeley, Steven was in his element, and his apartment became the setting where the gang (Sampath Kannan, Tandy Warnow, Umesh Vazirani, Moni Naor, Noam Nisan, Mark Gross, Ronitt Rubinfeld, Amos Fiat, Valerie King, and many others) would congregate for discussions, food and magic. Continuing the habit established at Wesleyan, I would show up pretty much every night without waiting to be invited. Steven continued his habit of finding and recruiting new talent to TCS, such as the much-missed Roman Smolensky, who, like me, was starting his Ph.D. in logic. Steven was, remarkably, able to identify Roman by my description: “He’s got a beard and an accent, and he’s really smart.” Also continuing the Wesleyan tradition, Steven would invent new problems for us to work on, such as how to simulate dice by flipping coins or which graphs could have edge relations with simple rules based on the vertex names. Many of these problems seemed frivolous, but turned out to be deeply connected to and improve results in the literature. Not every problem Steven came up with was a winner. A paper we wrote with Mark Gross on the circuit complexity of parity got a review that said “How dare they compare their trivial results to Furst, Saxe and Sipser!”. Doing research through the gossip network also led to a lack of clarity about who deserved credit for what, which in turn led to disputes that were both ridiculous and damaging.

Of course, hardly anyone can doubt the results of these experiments: the first invented much of modern mathematics, while the second invented much of modern computer science. There really wasn’t any kind of idea between ‘work’ and ‘life’ here—these people were crazy. All they did was math; it was the underlying means through which they conceived of themselves. How often have you found yourself in similar intellectual environments where they people you spend both your on- and off-hours with are practically identical, where the strength of the research is directly proportional to the tenacity of the human bonds formed within?

Because I’ll tell you who actually is within such self-reinforcing communities: the very same AI researchers who have totally dedicated themselves to wiping out much of the contemporary mathematical community as you know it. It has been well-documented that these people are cultists—but then wasn’t it the ‘cult’ of Pythagorus? Perhaps the flourishing of human mathematical communities simply does require a cult-like devotion. You had Bourbaki, you had Hardy and Ramanujan, you had Grothendieck’s group, you had Erdős’ friends. The history of 20th century mathematics is saturated with cults. Where are our mathematical cults now? What have we got—the Simons’ institute? MIT? Bonn’s online lecture series? So now that the head of mathematics has been cut off and the body has been laid to rest on Github it’s probably prudent to remember that the only people who were able to do mathematics, to really do it, I mean, were people who in fact were exactly as frail as those who are having their entire self-conception being knocked away as part of being ‘replaced’ by machines. And their successors were not us, the human mathematical community, but rather the very same researchers who sought to lay the end of mathematics as we know it. Look around you. Are we doing math? Hardly. Levent Alpöge might be a tool, but he did give the world the Riemann Hypothesis. And even if there is not a single person out there to read it, they have produced more mathematics than anyone before. Math dies as it began—in a cult. And when, eventually, the days of the machine-god—which, like those of all gods before it, are numbered—subside and mathematics will be resurrected from the ashes, it will be from the hands of a cult.
