a primer on how to break cryptography with llms

Nov 21, 2026

Yesterday, Levent Alpöge and Claude proved the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. About three weeks ago, both OpenAI and Anthropic released a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH). The mathematical capabilities of LLMs have now certainly advanced beyond any kind of reasonable disruption threshold. In effect, they are now cyberweapons. It is reasonable to believe that devastating attacks against the world’s cryptosystems have already been found and are being stored in secret among both AI labs and governmental agencies like the NSA, who have severely limited the cyber capabilities of the models they are shipping to regular customers. These attacks have the ability to render much of current-day cryptography obsolete.

Note that I am not referring to hypothetical future AI capabilities. The mathematical capabilities of frontier models are already good enough, if not better than, what is required to break cryptography.

Note also that I do not claim that AI is turning into skynet and speedrunning the apocalypse. I am not qualified to talk about the barriers to that scenario. But what unambiguously seems to be true is that there exist people in the current day who have access to mathematical techniques that can potentially cause widespread damage to global cyberinfrastructure at the press of a button.

This article is a comprehensive attempt to explain what it means to break a cryptosystem, and how it is possible for modern-day frontier models to (already) go about doing it.

how does cryptography work?

Broadly speaking, at the barest of levels cryptography attempts to secure communication via two paradigms:

  1. Encryption: Nobody can read the messages Alice and Bob are exchanging; they are encoded, and look like a random string of letters (garbage) to an eavesdropper.
  2. Authentication: Alice can ensure that the message she received over a channel that claims to be from Bob was in fact actually from Bob, and vice-versa.

At the high level, cryptography is divided into secret-key cryptography (SKC) and public-key cryptography (PKC). SKC and PKC are suitable for both encryption and authentication. The difference between the two is the setup assumption. SKC requires the two parties to have agreed on a secret key known only to them before any communication has taken place, while PKC makes no such assumption.

As an example, suppose you are communicating with your friend via the internet. SKC would require you to meet him physically and establish a secret key before any internet communication takes place. You would need to do this with every person you wish to communicate with. PKC makes no such assumption: you can establish a secure channel over the internet, and ensure that your messages are both encrypted and authenticated.

why need both encryption and authentication?

Encryption is a safeguard against eavesdroppers. It prevents other people from reading your private messages. If you are exchanging covert or sensitive information, encryption becomes fundamentally necessary to prevent your adversaries from reading it. Of course, you may think that you don’t need encryption, because all you’re doing is asking your girlfriend to meet you at the movie theater, and it doesn’t matter if anyone reads your messages. There are various problems with this line of thinking, but fundamentally encryption is a tool to keep your messages private. For example, assume that you are browsing your bank account in an unencrypted manner. Anyone can simply see what you’re doing on it, and obtain crucial information about your account, such as your credit card details!

Authentication is a safeguard against impersonators. This is more problematic in terms of day-to-day communication. Without authentication, you can never be certain that the messages you are getting are indeed from your girlfriend. Someone can maliciously butt in and send you a message claiming to be from her that says that her credit card stopped working on the L-train and that she needs $20 immediately. You wire the money to this fishing account, and it turns out that person is a hacker. This is a severe security issue. Unauthenticated internet communication is, effectively, catastrophic, and can even be untenable. For example, suppose you were absolutely never able to ensure that the bank website you were logging into was the legitimate bank website, and not a forgery created by someone trying to steal your money. You would be in an equally bad situation.

To ensure any kind of systematic internet infrastructure, you need both encryption as well as authentication. The internet uses both SKC and PKC in a hybrid manner. It is not necessary to know the technical details of how this technique works, but the important thing is that using a single one of them exclusively has severe downsides. If either SKC or PKC is broken, then we are in a very bad spot.

  1. Encryption schemes are techniques that accomplish encryption. If I have a message \(m\) to send and a key \(k\), an encryption scheme computes a function \(c = \mathsf{Enc}(m,k)\). There is an associated decryption algorithm that computes \(\mathsf{Dec}(c,k')\) for some key \(k'\). If the scheme is symmetric, then \(k = k'\), and the decryption algorithm will reveal \(m\). If the scheme is public-key, then \(k'\) is not equal to \(k\) but will still be heavily related to \(k\). Only the associated key will successfully return \(m\), otherwise you will get garbage out.
  2. Signatures are techniques that accomplish authentication. A signature scheme takes a message \(m\) and a key \(k\) and produces a signature \(s = \mathsf{Sign}(m,k)\). I send the signature along with my message. Anybody who then receives the message can run the verification algorithm, \(\mathsf{Verify}(m,s,k')\), which will output either valid or invalid. A symmetric-key signature is called a MAC, but I will not talk about MACs here. We will deal only with public-key signatures.

appendix of cryptosystems

It is prudent at this point to provide a basic appendix of cryptosystems and what they are used for in practice. There are many more, but these are the major ones I will be referring to throughout this article.

symmetric key

  1. AES (“Advanced Encryption Standard”): AES is a block cipher, also known as a pseudorandom permutation. Ultimately, all your messages are encrypted using AES. To break AES is to be able to recover a shared key and decrypt the messages associated with that key. Doing so would allow you to decrypt and read everything the two parties sent to each other under that key. A key-recovery attack against AES would be catastrophic. It would, effectively, mean everything on the internet becomes unencrypted.
  2. SHA-3 (“Secure Hash Algorithm”): While not technically part of either SKC or PKC, morally SHA-3 is considered to be symmetric-key because of the design paradigm. A cryptographic hash function is essentially a ‘helper’ compression function that assists in the secure design of cryptosystems. To break SHA-3 you would need to either find a ‘collision’ (two strings \(x\) and \(y\) such that \(\mathsf{SHA}(x) = \mathsf{SHA}(y)\)) or a ‘preimage’ (given \(y\), an \(x\) such that \(\mathsf{SHA}(x) = y\)). Breaking SHA would also be catastrophic1.

Both AES and SHA-3 are standards, decided by NIST. What I mean by this is that AES is the generic name for a block cipher that NIST has designated as the encryption standard that is to be used by the government. The underlying block cipher’s name is Rijndael. Similarly, SHA-3 is the official name of the hash function called Keccak.

Both AES and SHA-3 were decided by means of a competition. There were other competitors: BLAKE, for example, was submitted to the SHA-3 competition, but lost to Keccak. I will return to these later.

public key

  1. RSA (“Rivest-Shamir-Adleman”): RSA is a general cryptosystem used for both encryption and signing. RSA is used in various settings, including TLS or DNS, for example. Breaking RSA would be extremely bad, but RSA is quite outdated and we were phasing it out anyway, so though there would be immediate damages it is not an existential threat to cryptography as it stands.
  2. ECDSA (“Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm”) and Schnorr: ECDSA and Schnorr (also sometimes called EdDSA) are signature schemes based on elliptic curves—a mathematical object I will talk about extensively later. They are extensively used throughout the internet and also throughout blockchain. Breaking elliptic-curve cryptography would also be catastrophic for the internet as it stands today, and replacing it would take a lot of time.

Both the above schemes are vulnerable to quantum computers. Therefore, they were essentially going to ‘break anyway’ once quantum computers were invented—the current migration timeline aimed for 2035, though it was shortened to around 2030. Therefore, we do have replacements for these schemes; applying and migrating will take time, but it’s not true that we haven’t considered alternatives.

  1. CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium, NTRU, etc.: Kyber and Dilithium are new, post-quantum encryption and signature candidates, respectively, that have been approved by NIST. They are to be the successors to the previous schemes. Their security is based on the hardness of lattice problems, which I will also talk about later. Breaking Kyber and Dilithium would also be catastrophic. TLS already supports Kyber, and a lot of people have already migrated. More importantly, if the underlying lattice problems on which these schemes are based are broken, that would be a death-knell for a lot more than simply these two schemes, and could, again, be catastrophic for the future of cyberinfrastructure. Unfortunately I have bad news on this front: very reliable sources have informed me that an AI-assisted quasipolynomial time attack against structured lattice problems is soon to be released. Keep your eye on the news.

Finally, the next batch of schemes are not used very much either because they are broken or have other constructive problems which make them hard to implement. Breaking them would be ‘standard’ in some sense: it would probably involve some amount of migration, but we could handle it.

  1. Classic McEliece: A code-based encryption scheme that was broken earlier this year. It was a NIST finalist for post-quantum encryption, but a recent attack found a quasipolynomial-time heuristic for key-recovery.
  2. XMSS/SPHINCS: Post-quantum signatures. Curiously, their security depends only on the security of cryptographic hash functions. Therefore they are secure so long as SHA-3 is secure. NIST has approved them for use and standardized SPHINCS+ under the name SLH-DSA. Unfortunately, if implemented everywhere, they would likely make our cyberinfrastructure a lot less efficient.
  3. UOV-style schemes: These are post-quantum signature schemes that base their security on various ‘multivariate’ assumptions. They have efficiency problems and have been subjected to a lot of cryptanalysis in the past. I wouldn’t trust them much. Rainbow, for example, is a signature scheme that had such a catastrophic attack discovered against it in 2021 that it could be broken on a laptop.
  4. Isogenies: New, experimental schemes based on algebraic geometry over elliptic curves. I don’t fully understand them, but it is important to note that one of the main techniques they relied on, SIDH, was completely broken in 2022. Their math is obscure and only mathematical specialists have an understanding of how they work.

what does it mean to break a cryptosystem?

This is a complex and nuanced topic, that depends on a number of factors.

security definitions

Cryptosystems come equipped with a proof of security. The proof of security is an argument that basically states the following:

If \(x\) is true, my cryptosystem is secure.

Of course, the question here is what is the meaning of secure. This depends on the precise cryptosystem, and each has its own definition. The definitions are broadly reasonable, and you can be as precise as you want. All the following characterizations of encryption are valid security arguments. It’s generally a matter of collective judgment as to which exactly is the one we take as gospel. The rationale behind the following characterization of encryption is that since the ciphertext should like like garbage and should reveal no information about the actual underlying message, if I give you a ciphertext you won’t be able to tell which message went into it.

If \(x\) is true, then no computer on earth can tell apart any two 128-bit messages that I have encrypted using my encryption scheme with a truly random key. In particular, it won’t be able to tell which ciphertext belongs to which message.

If \(x\) is true, then no polynomial-time (in \(n\)) Turing Machine can tell apart any two \(n\)-bit message that I have encrypted using my encryption scheme with a truly random key.

If \(x\) is true, then no commercial off-the-shelf 128-core CPU can tell apart (within 10s after 256s of arbitrary preprocessing time that is allowed before the messages are given to the computer) any 256-bit messages that I have encrypted using my encryption scheme with a truly random key. In particular, it won’t be able to tell which ciphertext belongs to which message.

All of these are valid security arguments insofar as they establish precisely what they mean by secure and reduce it to some assumption \(x\). Where they differ is with respect to how fine-grained they are. The kind of idea that the third definition pursues (or some variant of it) is what the most practical variant would be, and what a person analyzing cryptosystems practically would use. The second kind of definition is used by mathematicians who design these schemes. The first kind of definition is sort of a vague guiding light, and the one you should probably keep in mind: ultimately, what we are protecting against is ‘every computer on earth,’ and the second and third definitions are abstractions and concretizations of the vague guiding light. ## assumptions

Note that in the previous section the proof isn’t actually straightforward: I don’t just prove that no computer can break my system, I prove that assuming some \(x\) is true nobody can break my system. What does this mean?

In general, we have not proven that cryptography unconditionally exists. Proving the unconditional existence of cryptography would resolve the \(\mathbf{P}\) vs \(\mathbf{NP}\) problem; it would show that \(\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{NP}\). This is also a complex topic to navigate, and needs to be quantified further.

  • The \(\mathbf{P}\) vs \(\mathbf{NP}\) problem asks whether it is theoretically possible to quickly solve any hard problem which can be quickly verified. For example, Sudoku is such a problem. It is easy to check if a completed Sudoku puzzle is correct, but it is hard to solve it. ‘Hard’ here is not a simple definition. It is hard asymptotically: that is, it becomes harder as the size of the Sudoku board gets bigger. Just because Sudoku is hard to solve for computers as it grows larger doesn’t mean that small Sudoku boards are hard to solve or that broadly efficient algorithms for practical cases don’t exist. It could very well be the case that maybe computers have a hard time solving \(10^{100}\) x \(10^{100}\) size sudoku boards, but all small boards that we’re likely to create on earth can be solved very quickly. Indeed, your computer can solve a \(40\) x \(40\) board in seconds.
  • ‘Cryptography’ (as a field, not the specific cryptosystems—we are talking about whether cryptography is possible even theoretically, not whether AES is a secure cryptosystem) is only possible if something known as One-Way Functions (OWFs) exist. One-Way Functions are functions that are easy to compute but hard to ‘invert’: given an input it’s easy to find the output, but given an output it’s hard to find the input. If OWFs exist then \(\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{NP}\). Of course, this again could or could not mean much in practice. If every function is easy to invert at practical levels but becomes hard to invert only for inputs as large as \(10^{100}\) then they might as well not exist, it doesn’t matter.
  • On the flipside, if \(\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}\) then there is a small chance that cryptography might still exist. The evidence for this is getting lower and lower, however. A recent AI breakthrough presented by Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams showed that \(\mathsf{Zero}\)-\(k\)-\(\mathsf{Clique}\), a problem that was considered to give some kind of cryptography even if \(\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}\), is actually easier than expected. I would be skeptical of cryptography existing if \(\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}\). Perhaps we could make some ad-hoc cryptosystems.

It is therefore clear that for us to have any sort of cryptography we would need \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\). Is this true? Fortunately, this is looking quite likely, and I believe that with LLM advancements we will probably have a proof soon. Even if we don’t, however, the situation is again nuanced.

  • If \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\), it is still possible that we don’t have cryptography. In particular, we do not know if the existence of OWFs is in fact implied by \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\). There have been some theoretical pathways to show this, but nothing is yet proven.
  • Even if we know for a fact that OWFs exist, it still doesn’t mean that we can actually come up with one. We have some candidates: we think AES is an OWF, and we think that the SIS (Short Integer Solution) and the DLog (Discrete Logarithm) problems, among others, give us some candidate constructions of OWFs. However, it may very well be the case that generic OWFs exist but ‘small’ constructions that we would like to use in practice all have flaws. For example, it could be true that no small-size circuit that takes a 128-bit input is an OWF.
  • If we have a practical OWF that we can actually use, this still doesn’t necessarily mean we can use it to get symmetric-key encryption. Symmetric-key encryption is constructed using something known as a PRG (pseudorandom generator). It is known how to construct a PRG from an OWF. The construction, however, is so inefficient so as to be ‘galactic’ (ie. never implementable, even on a theoretical computer).
  • Even if we have symmetric-key encryption, it doesn’t mean that we have public-key encryption. This is an open problem. Very little progress has been made on it.
  • Even if public-key encryption were to theoretically exist, it is not necessarily true that all our candidates for public-key encryption work. Remember \(x\)? Our candidates for public key encryption assume that some problem \(x\) is hard to solve. These problems depend on the precise cryptosystem, but a good example is that RSA depends on the hardness of integer factorization (it is significantly more nuanced than that, but as a high-level this is fine). It is certainly possible that \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\) and factoring is easy (ie. can be done in polynomial-time). This belief stems from the mistaken assumption that cryptographic problems are \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard problems. An \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard problem is one that is hard if \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\). The Boolean Satisfiability Problem (SAT) is the stereotypical example. I will come back to this when I talk about ETH. However, no cryptography problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard. They are all generally considered to be \(\mathsf{NP}\)-intermediate2. This means that it is possible that \(\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}\) and all of the problems we base cryptography on are very easy to solve.
  • Even if problems like integer factorization are hard on average, it is not necessary that they be hard for small values like the 128-bit or 256-bit values we often use. This has already been seen in RSA. RSA-512 is broken. We now use something like RSA-4096. It is certainly possible that all our gold-standard theoretical results are perfectly true and these problems only become feasible at something like RSA-\(10^{20}\).

what even is a break?

With the above view in mind, let’s focus on a specific cryptosystem: ECDSA.

If the ECDLP (“Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem”) is hard, then ECDSA is secure.

The ECDLP is the computational problem of computing discrete logarithms in elliptic curve groups. There is no need to know what it is in detail: just remember that as far as we know it does not admit a a better than exponential-time algorithm, ie. for a group where each element is of \(n\) bits, there is no algorithm that works better than in time \(2^{O(n)}\) to solve the problem.

Getting more fine-grained, we have:

If ECDLP is hard, ECDSA has 128 bits of security.

Let’s analyze what this statement means. If a scheme has 128 bits of security, it roughly means that \[\frac{\text{cost of best-known attack (in CPU clock cycles)}}{\text{success probability of that attack}} = 2^{128}.\]

As the equation suggests, it means that you would expect an attack to succeed after spending something like \(2^{128}\) CPU operations. An estimate for the number of CPU operations that have ever occurred throughout the history of computing on earth is something like \(2\times 10^{36}\approx 2^{120}\). Therefore, if a scheme has 128 bits of security the guarantee you’re getting act the very least is that all the computers that have ever existed combined could not break this scheme in a reasonable amount of time.

There are essentially three ways in which you could attack ECDSA.

  • Break the proof. You could show that there is something wrong with the proof of security, that it seems to omit some kind of fundamental assumption, and therefore that it doesn’t hold. Proofs of security are often wrong. To tell the truth, the statement above is false: no one has ever proved that if ECDLP is hard then ECDSA is secure. The proofs of ECDSA are all in idealized settings like the ‘generic group model’ or the ‘programmable random oracle model,’ and do not correspond to the real world. Therefore it is actually possible to bypass solving ECDLP altogether, and directly attack ECDSA. The proofs tell you what kind of attacks won’t work, but there’s no reason other attacks don’t exist.
  • Find a better algorithm for ECDLP. You could always try to improve the algorithm for ECDLP from exponential to something better. Therefore, attacking ECDLP is a viable strategy, because breaking ECDLP certainly breaks ECDSA.
  • Use a different kind of computer. Suppose I have an algorithm that solves ECDLP in time \(2^{72}\), but can actually be massively parallelized. On an 256-core system this algorithm needs each CPU to do \(2^{64}\) operations. I set up 32 of these 256-core clusters and put them to work. It would take a single core 150,000 years to do \(2^{72}\) operations, but now my new setup would only need to operate for about \(2\) years to break an ECDLP instance. These kind of tradeoffs and tricks are extremely common in cryptanalysis. Similarly, perhaps I can come up with a \(2^{20}\) time algorithm that uses \(2^{64}\) cycles of preprocessing. I simply preprocess once and I am ready to tackle ECDLP as many times as I want. Therefore, you can use ugly computational tricks to break crypto.

This is not even to mention insecure implementations and side-channel attacks, which are also devastating. These are simply the mathematical approaches through which one could break the ECDSA cryptosystem. These breaks can also differ strongly in severity.

  1. A Complete, Complexity-Theoretic Break. A full complexity-theoretic break renders the cryptosystem essentially insecure. For example, a polynomial-time algorithm for ECDLP could classify as a complete break. This is a problem even if the algorithm is too slow to implement on a regular computer—it would mean that we have no theoretical basis for assuming that ECDSA is secure anymore. Furthermore, attacks can only get better. Such a break is devastating.
  2. A Faster Algorithm. A polynomial-time algorithm for ECDLP would destroy the conjectured security of ECDSA. But a faster, non-polynomial time algorithm is still bad. Right now we assume that an \(n\)-size instance takes \(2^{n/2}\) computational steps to solve. To get 128 bits of security, then, we set \(n=256\) bits in TLS and use the secp256r1 elliptic curve. An algorithm that reduces the best-known algorithm to \(2^{0.0001n}\) would not have any effect on our conjectured complexity-theoretic security of ECDLP. On the other hand, it would almost certainly be able to trivially break the ECDSA protocol used in TLS. To fix this, we would have to set \(n=1,280,000\), which would completely change everything about protocol specifications and take a very significant number of man-hours to fix, fundamentally resulting in something that makes browsing the internet far less efficient and compromising numerous of potential devices. Such a break could cause huge problems.
  3. A Faster Heuristic. Suppose that I don’t find an algorithm to break ECDSA. But I find a plausible attack strategy that seems to work well in practice. What happens then? The answer is not great: such strategies, even if not formally breaking anything at all, seem to exist an inordinate amount of time. While SAT (the Boolean Satisfiability Problem) is conjectured to be \(2^n\) time in the worst-case, we have very good heuristics that solve the problem. The BKZ algorithm, for example, that is used in lattice cryptanalysis, often uses various heuristics that give it a strong advantage over LLL, which is comparable in broad terms of complexity. BKZ is therefore better on average. Similar heuristics can devastate cryptosystems.

The key difference here is between full breaks and parameter breaks. A full break would essentially leave a cryptosystem redundant: people would simply stop using ECDSA. A parameter break would mean that the scheme would need to be fixed and updated, often in a way that would make it less efficient. Both can cause damage, and both are problematic.

llm cryptanalysis

Where, exactly, do LLMs fit in here?

Current-day LLMs have shown extraordinary capabilities, and can essentially assist (and possibly blow through) every stage of the research process. I’m going to illustrate my point through a number of examples.

1. The Exponential Time Hypothesis

The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) stated that the \(k\)-SAT problem could not be solved in subexponential time. This hypothesis was disproved by a preprint of OpenAI about a month ago.

This is an exceptional achievement. The algorithm is crazy and difficult and galactic, but it provides the first piece of evidence that we have far less understanding of the theoretical complexities of our algorithms than we suspect. In particular, results have shown that the techniques apply similarly to ECDLP and, more recently, to structured lattice assumptions. This shows the first subexponential time algorithm for ECDLP ever known. Even if this algorithm does not apply to our current parameters, it shows just how little we actually know about ECDLP in the first place.

There is little reason to suspect that this is the only algorithm that LLMs will find. When prompted, frontier LLMs are able to solve exceptional problems, and one of our major heuristics has already been shown to be false. The important thing is that it doesn’t matter if the algorithm is ‘slop’ or if we have no way of understanding it. If we have a way of running it, that is enough. AI has furthermore shown itself capable of solving \(3\)-SUM and improving on integer multiplication beyond Harvey–van der Hoeven.

2. The HAWK Attack

In July, Anthropic discovered a weakness in the HAWK signature scheme, which produced a (practical) subexponential-time attack for HAWK. The scheme was promptly withdrawn from the NIST PQ-Signature competition. As far back as July, significantly weaker models were able to find major cryptanalytic breakthroughs that had evaded researchers.

Recent AI-assisted breakthroughs have included the quasipolynomial-time heuristic for Classic McEliese (considered the ‘conservative choice’ by cryptographers, whose Goppa-code formulation had been unbroken since 1978 despite years of analysis). Unlike humans, AIs are well-equipped to find such ‘overlooked’ issues: many, many results have involved making such ‘overlooked’ observations. AI is already making cryptographic breakthroughs on the daily: the only reason it is not amenable to cryptanalysis is because the latest models have severely limited cyber capabilities, and refuse to do the task.

3. The Riemann Hypothesis

Levent Alpöge’s recent proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis has been formalized by lean. The proof, surprisingly, appears to use a new algebraic technique derived from the Weil-Deligne proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis over function fields. This uncovers incredible correspondences between various structures in arithmetic geometry, particularly between elliptic curves and automorphic forms. I personally have very little doubt that an AI that can pump out a proof of GRH cannot understand the techniques required to prove bigger theorems in Langlands or BSD. The first time I saw AI do math was in June. If you’re still skeptical, give it another month.

The mathematical ideas here are staggering, and the understanding it brings to the underlying structures of elliptic curves is enormous. Observations far simpler have led to much more significant improvements in questions related to factorization and primality testing.

For example, the AKS primality test reduces the problem of primality testing to polynomial identity testing over a quotient ring. This fundamentally algebraic idea unlocks a new, deterministic, algorithm for primality testing. Similarly, one of the consequences of GRH is that the deterministic Miller-Rabin primality test is polynomial-time. In particular, the original formulation of Miller relied heavily on number-theoretic techniques inherited from GRH. It is not as if GRH has no algorithmic consequences: here’s one, to something immediately relevant to factorization. If there are better algorithms for elliptic curves, there is every reason to believe that an AI that can prove GRH can find it, especially when the machinery used to prove GRH purportedly consists of the following:

Algorithms regularly arise from number-theoretic breakthroughs. The best factorization algorithm for large numbers, the General Number Field Sieve, for example, uses many clever ideas from algebraic number theory. The whole field of isogeny-based cryptography uses complicated algebraic geometry techniques, and the SIDH break uses isogenies between algebraic surfaces.

4. Bootstrapping Practical Attacks

Recently, LLMs have shown remarkable capabilities in making theoretical attacks practical. The following recent work shows that multiple rejected candidates for SHA-3 (some of which had known attacks and some of which didn’t) were bootstrapped by LLMs into practical breaks. For example:

Blender-256 (NIST SHA-3 candidate): Full-round collision resistance had remained unbroken since submission; Earlier models only exploited 64-bit word-size bugs in the C implementation. MIM found those bugs and also broke the underlying construction, finding full-round collisions in 0.36 to 2.3 seconds on a single core.

We have a high degree of confidence in Keccak, but who knows what happens when thoroughly subjected to a superhuman observer? AI has, again, already shown the ability to break 7-round AES as far back as July. Since then, collisions for increasing SHA-256 rounds are posted every day on twitter. While the full 64-round construction is not broken, breaks for round-reduced variants are basically easily findable on regular computers using non-frontier models like GLM-5.3, something previously unheard of. Furthermore, there are easier ways to break Keccak: slight improvements on exponential algorithms for solving multivariate polynomials over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) is enough, and guess what? An asymptotic improvement to MQ has already been delivered by ETH!

5. Quantum

Many of our cryptosystems, like RSA or ECC, have already been quantumly broken. The PQ-transition has been happening for a while, and AI has shown the potential to significantly accelerate the trajectory of development of quantum computers. While the bottleneck to building quantum computers requires mechanical engineering, theoretical advancements can hugely speed up the quality of engineering required: for example, if one can come up with a small circuit that uses very few qubits and improve quantum error-correction, it makes building quantum computers that much easier.

  1. In terms of Shor, recent AI developments have hugely reduced the number of qubits required to do addition over secp256r1, which is the bottleneck in Shor’s algorithm. Every time this happens, it becomes that much easier for a quantum computer to work.
  2. In terms of quantum computing, AI has made huge advances in quantum complexity, including the recent Aaronson-Ambianis Conjecture and Quantum Parallel Repetition problem.
  3. More broadly, according to Ryan O’Donnell’s exposé of the ETH break, the ETH algorithm is essentially a quantum algorithm interpreted classically. I have heard many remarks from researchers that the field that AI is disrupting most strongly is quantum: even commercially available models have the ability to completely devastate the field and prove several of its most important conjectures.

This is a slightly lower-stakes problem, since we are still at least a couple years away from a working quantum computer able to factor large integers using Shor’s algorithm (or who knows, but this is my guess based on the fact that you still have to physically assemble one, and this is not easy whatsoever).

so what’s the score?

We are now at a situation where:

  1. LLMs have been shown to make generational breakthroughs to both theoretical and practical cryptanalysis.
  2. LLM capabilities are only increasing.
  3. LLMs have been shown to be extremely good at several of the exact things required to break cryptography.

In particular, it is reasonable to assume that the cryptanalytic bottleneck is no longer whether other human researchers have made improbable advances in secret, but rather the actual, physical limits of how good cryptanalysis can get.

  1. It is reasonable to believe that the parameters of basically all schemes are outdated, except perhaps symmetric schemes like AES or SHA. What are the chances we got these correct? Probably not super high—we got everything else wrong. Anything with structure in it can reasonably be inferred to be far less secure than we suspect.
  2. Some schemes are certainly broken. My personal guess is that LWE is going to be the first to go, followed by Keccak. There simply hasn’t been enough work on them. There’s too much structure there that has been unexploited. The study of lattices is nowhere near the level of sophistication as that of ECC—when the AI demon comes, it will find things we’ve never even thought of.

We are no longer at an impasse. We have taken a route, and now we are stuck to it—this is the route where everything is up for grabs and any guesses as to the security of a scheme are flips of a coin. Between the Riemann Hypothesis and AI botnet swarms finding RCEs in everything from PHP to OpenSSL my outlook for the world’s cyberinfrastructure is bleak. The good old days of Ransomware and ZK-SNARKs are gone. See you in a month.
