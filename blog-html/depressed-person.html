and so we find ourselves here again where it all started

Sep 15, 2026

One of the things that is so slippery and yet evocative about the work of David Foster Wallace is that when he attempts to address the psychology of people who have been exposed to modernity he often addresses their psychologies as a whole, whatever that comprises of, and sometimes his attempt to address these psychologies comes off sounding much less diagnostic and clinical and closer to what can be perceived as cruel. It is true that Wallace is often cruel, and it is true that Wallace is often clinical. But in that sense his batting average with respect to only subjecting those who are actually deserving of his mirth to it is quite high, even if it takes a little bit of time to actually perceive it as so—which happened to me with his short story The Depressed Person, one that I categorically rejected the first time I encountered it on qualms of it being far too uncomfortable of an experience to actually flip through, but which since then I have periodically revisited until now when reading it is nearly always a transformative experience. Like most of Wallace’s work, it feels like it has the secrets of the universe hidden within it.1

I guess what takes a while to actually understand and internalize about The Depressed Person is that it requires you to sit with a rather uncomfortable truth that, like a lot of Wallace’s work, is then reflected back on the reader instead of the head of the person we are currently inhabiting—who, in this case, turns out to be depressed, for some measure of depressed. Because it is called The Depressed Person you’d like to think that the story is about depression, but it really isn’t, despite the fact that it does often form the same kind of projection exercise that Good Old Neon does. I think of myself as very much like Neal from Good Old Neon; I even phrase things the same way he does, in his unique blend of preppy and regular-guy lowbrow, but unlike him it the accusations and feeling of being a fraud—or really what’s more like a fundamental inability to inhabit the world you are in now, instead of perceiving things via this third person perspective of someone who is judging the person you are within the body that you inhabit, you who can’t even lose yourself within the arms of the pretty girl at school because you’re so neurotic about being the person who finally found himself within those said arms—that feeling of being a fraud is something that I know all-too-well but unlike Neal does not cause me any kind of discomfort, it’s just there, present, ‘coolio isn’t it great that she’s right here next to me, that I’m the guy who’s in her arms’ without the aforementioned sadness that I’m thinking that instead of fully experiencing or being in the moment, whatever that means. I could lament not being in the moment, but after all, I am simply myself and can’t be anyone else, no matter how hard I may try, so not-really-being-in-the-moment is just something that I do, and that is what comprises being-in-the-moment for me after all. There’s a lot more processing there than just that simple aphorism; I’m sure there are quite a number of clicks and wires going off in that brain of mine, but whenever that happens it doesn’t hit the ‘and I hate myself for it’ part of the brain and so I can read Good Old Neon and feel seen and experience a kind of catharsis despite not finding myself looking for a toilet bowl to puke in midway through because I saw something I didn’t like in those pages.

The Depressed Person is a bit more like that. As I said, I didn’t like it at first. It was painful and bewildering to read precisely because the usual writerly empathy that Wallace gives to Kate Gompert and her desire to jump off a burning building in Infinite Jest is absent and replaced with almost a sneering, wallowing sense of cruelty. Wallace, it seems, does not actually like the depressed person, even as he obviously feels some sense of pity towards her.

It is hard to reconcile these two things, at least for me. It is easy to me to empathize with the depressed person, not only because I have experienced several of those emotions firsthand off-and-on during various times of my life but also because he includes several of those little tidbits that serve to humanize someone by presenting episodes of their life in a manner that makes you feel a kind of pitiable sadness inside, the kind of empathy you feel when you hear about a little boy who invited his whole class to his birthday party and sat at the table with his hat and balloons and the hours ticking by while the slow realization that no one was going to arrive creeped upon him. The depressed person’s parents got divorced and they wouldn’t even buy her retainers, for instance, and when they did it was some kind of divorce-related power play. What kind of an effect that would have on a young girl!

Stories like those, in isolation, can paint the picture of someone who has been subjected repeatedly to negative experiences in their life leading to what psychologists call ‘accumulated trauma.’ Because Wallace is writing about modernity, he is interested in the way that ‘accumulated trauma’ manifests in modernity, with all the nice techniques and gizmos we have to thoroughly psychoanalyze ourselves and understand, of course, that the source of our traumas are often rooted in the fundamental aspects of our childhood, and that addressing their ‘root causes’ involves quite a great deal of back-and-forth with professionals that help you better understand how to deal with the behaviors you have imbibed by these traumas. Whenever I think of things like this it reminds me of a story that an old friend of my dad’s told me while digging through a stash of boxes in his garage during a clean-up drive. One of the boxes sidelined at the very end contained some of his effects from when he was a high-school boy, all the way back in rural India, and they contained a hefty number of comic books along with several illustrations he himself had made which were quite sophisticated for a high school boy and wove themselves into coherent and funny storylines. I asked him if he used to make comics as a kid. There was the familiar wonder in his eyes of a man recollecting fond memories that had not been summoned for almost as long a period as had passed since their formation. Then he laughed and told me about how he used to draw these all the time back in ninth or tenth grade and how sometimes the kids used to make fun of him, going so far as to even tear a few of his preciously made comics and throw them into the trash. It was quite a horrible little memory, and for a second I did not really know what was coming next. Then he threw his head back and laughed widely, as if it were something really funny, and when I asked him if he had stopped after that he said hell no, he didn’t even remember who the boys were and had simply glued them together and finally only stopped drawing them the next year after his interest petered out.

What I’m trying to get at is that it’s kind of amazing to me that he laughed.

In retrospect it shouldn’t be. But then we live in a world driven by ressentiment and seeing people who are so obviously free of the things that burden several of those among us is kind of a surreal experience. It feels hard to imagine that a person who has obviously been subjected to what by definition is a kind of childhood traumatic experience can laugh so freely about it while being entirely unaffected by an episode that by any psychoanalytic measure should have been imprinted upon his psyche along with innumerable other such reinforcing experiences that serve only to erode his self-esteem.

Or rather what I’m getting at is that the depressed person’s depression stemmed from a fundamental accumulation of such psychoanalytic traumas that it basically rendered her incapable of doing anything because she was caught up in her head about said traumas. And this could of course be true, but it doesn’t explain away the fact that there are millions of kids in America whose parents have been divorced and who were also subjected to such divorce-games as the depressed person and yet those people are not depressed and can laugh away these traumas as merely an event that occurred to them and something that does not really occupy their psyche to any significant degree at all, and only appears to them at times with the kind of annoying fortitude that doing their taxes also holds.

At this point I should mention that this entire line of thinking—and this post, too—stems from the diatribes of this blogger am I exposed to that never cease to mention irrelevantia about how they accumulated traumas from successive rejections from women and the bullying that follows, leading to what obviously are discreet sexual hangups but also a general feeling of self-righteousness along with proselytizing rationalist tendencies that are very relevant to this new batch of LLM nonsense that we are being subjected to doesn’t seek to do anything but undermine the foundational instincts upon which our field is founded. That was a bit of a diversion, but ultimately serves merely as another illustration of how people these days, especially people who are intelligent and/or susceptible, tend to structure themselves around these understandings of subtle traumatic experiences that they can never fully let go of.

And I guess the point that Wallace is trying to make is that everyone has suffered through these experiences. It’s easy to look at the world around you with a moral lens and categorize people into bullies and those who are ‘superior’ in some sense (read: chads, ugh) one on hand and Houellebecq’s underclass on the other, feeling a healthy dose of ressentiment towards who are seemingly free from these hangups that have left the underclass so desolate and unhappy. But in reality that is not true. Everybody around you is a human being, and even those who are cruel and have hurt you have done so probably not out of a malicious impulse that you ascribe to them but rather because they didn’t really think things through at all and were merely reacting on impulse. Your problems are not unique. In fact, they probably aren’t even that bad—there are people out there who have similar trauma but it’s from the fact that they were molested as a child, and while it feels bad to turn to a person who has been expressing how small acts of cruelty have resulted their being unable to psychologically function in this modern environment and say that ‘to be honest, kids in Africa don’t even have water and you’re worrying about that one time you were fifteen and some girl made fun of you for being so fat your pants were bursting at the seams’ it nevertheless does not change the fact that it’s true. Kids in Africa actually do have it worse. And it sucks that you have had to go through those things but in fact that is exactly the kind of thing that everyone around you who seems psychologically healthy, actually, has indeed managed to get through to the point that it crosses their mind like once a year, if at all.

This is what Wallace is saying to the depressed person—that her problems aren’t caused by depression but caused by the self-wallowing tendencies of narcissism and if her solutions are going to come from anywhere they aren’t going to come from further recognition of the fundamentals of her own psyche and in fact further, recursive self-awareness that serves only to make her question more fundamentals about herself but rather from the simple recognition that her friend is a human being too and really needs to go and that if she wasn’t being so pathetic perhaps she actually could last in her room for a little bit without her friend over the telephone line. It sounds cruel, but this is what Wallace is in fact trying to say in This is Water. If you extend empathy to all people, then wouldn’t you extend empathy to your bullies, too?

Isn’t that a radical thought? Only so as the thought that the bible says that Jesus loved Hitler, too.

And in fact it is easy to extend empathy to all peoples except the ones that you hate and who have wronged you, but then it wouldn’t need to be said in that overly complex way that Wallace says it, wouldn’t it? In fact it only matters if you are extending empathy to the people that you hate, too, because by thinking of them not as personal villains out to get you and only you it serves you much better to think of them as just human beings, just like you, and that makes life a fair bit easier. Just as you have been subjected to torment, haven’t you ever been cruel to another? Haven’t you regretted it? There’s a good chance they probably do, too.

And that is Wallace’s unfortunate conclusion: being someone who is narcissistic, whatever good reason you have for it, doesn’t actually make you likable. Unfortunately, you do suck. And no one is going to coming to solve your problems for you. And those problems are perhaps both the most easy and the most difficult problems in the world to solve, as hard exactly as those who succumbed to them found it, and also as easy as the rest of the world did who never once had them in the first place. You’re just as human as they are, after all.

  1. By the way, you are only really going to understand what I write here if you have read some of Wallace, in particular the short stories The Depressed Person, This is Water, Good Old Neon and the novella Something to do With Paying Attention. They are some of the greatest writing I’ve ever read and certainly very much worth it. ↩
