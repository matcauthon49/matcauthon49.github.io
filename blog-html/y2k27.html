For a long time my conception of the future was shaped by a quote that I encountered sometime during my woeful years as a young teenager which has no particular source as far as I can remember—although during the course of writing this sentence I looked it up using the information avalanche that is this new ‘artificial intelligence model’ and apparently it is from an old Jacobin article from Sam Kriss, which sounds about right—it’s a quote about the movie Back to the Future II and in particular about how it effectively visualizes the kind of future that every post-war generation has always found themselves subjected to; one in which you have flying cars, but life is as shitty as ever. That is what I thought the future would always be like. Funny the things that stick with you—all those embarrassing moments from your childhood which involved you misunderstanding profoundly basic social obligations and random quotes which weaseled their way into your memory like a drill into a wall.

The ‘nothing ever happens’ future—that was my future. Our lives would be gradually upended by the rising tide of change, but it would be a tide, and it would be slow, and it was only ‘in retrospect’ that I would find myself completely overwhelmed by this tide during nostalgic moments of reflection; most of it would be work and commuting and things like that, and perhaps the car I would be taking would be a bit faster than it used to be but it would be a car, nevertheless, and it would be work, which I had to drive to, and there would be a home to return to, and there would be food to eat, and there would be a lawn and there would be grass on it and it would have to be mowed. Those things were always just going to be the same. My parents would grow old and I’d have to take care of them, I’d get a wife, in the end, she’d be pretty, and I’d have to go to the in-laws, and we’d take out a mortgage, and then I’d lose my job and worry about how I was ever going to pay it back but we’d find some kind of way to eventually do it. In saying so I cannot help but wonder how much of my life has been shaped by television—I was always a television kid, sitting in front of the screen for hours on end, rushing back home at 3PM from school to switch on Cartoon Network to watch the latest iteration of the Pokémon show (that too has ended now) and then eventually watching the hours fade away until some or the other little hooligan would invite me to ride bikes around the neighborhood and play soccer in this little patch of grass we had behind in a park that we’d drawn some lines on with chalk. Good days. I didn’t have a phone, then, of course, we got those a lot later—later I memorized a few of their landline numbers (I still have them with me, to this day, two-five-seven-four-four…) and I’d occasionally call to ask if you’d be free at five and I’d get the response no I’m free at six but it was winter and the sun would have set way before then and we’d never be able to play anything, not really, just walk around under the streetlamps talking about the stupid things happening at school.

A while ago I tried to write something about those days and in particular about what the future looked like in those days, and I came up with something resembling almost a vague, nostalgic monologue within a story otherwise full of ominousness:

All this took place, as I said, back in my senior year starting late 2014 […] The automotive industry had died out and was being replaced by big tech, and pretty much every humdrum suburban blue-collar worker was being shafted out to the very edges of the Detroit area or into rural Michigan proper while the influx of coastals in the North with their newly minted Camrys and ProQuest jobs were propping up both the city itself and the suburban lifestyle that went along with it. There were a lot more glasses and nervous twitching. Lions caps became increasingly ill-fitting. Suddenly you could walk into a Starbucks and see someone crouched over a MacBook. Mac Miller was on the radio […] unlike the other kids which were asked to rote-memorize multiplication tables Molly would instead spend time dissecting all manner of underwater fauna and cultivating the so-called ‘twenty-first century critical skills’ which had already become outdated by the end of that very decade. This was a time of general optimism in technological progress and kids like Molly were expected to be at the forefront of it. There were programs on the History Channel where grubby-looking scientists made claims like the first person to be immortal had already been born, and Molly to me certainly seemed like a good candidate.

But then that was exactly what it was like, wasn’t it? Sure we had our problems—there was the looming threat of global warming, but we’d fixed the ozone layer and so we were going to fix that as well; we used to calculate carbon footprints and turn off the faucets while brushing to save water, and we used to have these drives to clean up rivers, that sort of thing. What was that they used to say? ‘Every little bit matters.’ I do remember opening up the television and looking at the History Channel and if they ever stopped talking about ancient astronauts they might occasionally have Neil deGrasse Tyson on. This was before he was uncool. I used to watch reruns of Cosmos in the 2000s that we rented from the library and later he had a sequel show that everyone’s forgotten which used to air on National Geographic. He was no Carl Sagan, but he was pretty good, and he and all these other physics guys on TV who would talk about Dark Matter and Dark Energy and all these things got me really into learning some pop physics; I begged my parents to buy me some books for my birthday (I always got books on my birthday, we’d either go to the library and pick them up or get some off Amazon, which used to be subsidized and dirt cheap—it was a win-win for both of us, and it seems very quaint now how they would spend about $20 and manage to get ten of them and that’d comprise a whole birthday present; I was a good kid, I guess, I was plenty satisfied with that) and they got me all this Brian Greene and Michio Kaku stuff and they would talk about how in the future you wouldn’t have to shave or anything like that, some robot razor emerging from your mirror would do it for you, whatever. I used to watch YouTube videos about all the new iPhones and things like that coming out, and I was like twelve and thought the Google Glass was the next big thing (of course I didn’t understand anything like privacy). There were all these cool gadgets and gizmos that were supposed to come out. I remember when I first saw a Roomba, that was a big one. Always hated the name, it’s so gauche—Roomba, what are we, twelve—but I had seen my mother slave away cleaning these rooms every single day and was correctly skeptical that such a stupid thing would ever be able to do the task to her satisfaction, hell even I couldn’t. Later it simply turned out that my mother was kind of a clean freak, something which she imparted into me enough that I’m grateful; I still am a bit of a slob, but there’s enough nurtured programming in me that I do clean up after myself and set the bed on time.

There were a lot of things happening in those days. Physics wasn’t yet dead, not quite—the LHC had opened and they’d found Higgs, that was a big one, injected some much-needed juice into the whole enterprise. I remember it was on the first page of the Times. I used to read the newspaper back then, partly because my (enforced) bedtime was too early for late-night sports and partly because there would be the day’s TV listings on it, I couldn’t possibly miss the National Treasure re-run. And then before I knew it I went from a 9PM sleeper to a 12:30AM sleeper, but even during my worst days in college I wasn’t bad enough to be an all-nighter guy; I’d hit the bed by 2AM, 3 on a bad day. Never seemed worth it to compromise on sleep. But the idea of something like that being on the Times or there even being a Times seems so odd now. Every time I open the webpage it’s always something bizarre now—apparently Jensen Huang’s in therapy, that’s the title of the headliner I just opened. The ‘Hitler Dead’ one would’ve been a true wonder to witness, though I’m glad I wasn’t around for that. But I mean there were kids still into things like physics and innovation and so on—The Social Network movie came out and I was a dumb little shit and thought how cool it’d be to be Justin Timberlake’s Sean Parker in that one, and I learned programming for no good reason after watching that movie. It was some stupid programming, too, something to do with using ‘Arduinos’—one of those crappy IoT things that they used to market as the next big thing but was ultimately just another surveillance system all along. It wasn’t all sunshine and roses, of course, there was also stuff about the ‘deep web’ and I used to think that Bitcoin was something used by evil internet drug dealers and hitmen instead of just a plain old scam—for a while there the past couple years I was really trying to patch up threshold signatures for blockchain bros, wasn’t I? I wouldn’t have known I was going to become a cryptographer, not really; I kind of always wanted to, ever since I knew about Alan Turing and the enigma, but then how many people actually end up becoming the things they’ve always wanted to be? I had a friend who wanted to be a television writer and now he’s in management consultancy, and another who wanted to be an academic philosopher who last I heard used to write SQL for a living.

But then in retrospect I couldn’t have been anything other than a cryptographer, I was so temperamentally suited to it. Even as I was hooked on computers there was a part of me that hated them. I understood them too well, too quickly; I knew what the internet was, I knew how to clear my browsing history, I knew how to write scripts and how to clear caches, I knew how to use the linux kernel, I knew how Tor worked, and yet I was the kind of person who romanticized nothing more than sitting on a large oakwood chair and sipping a little bit of coffee and thinking of L-functions while looking out the window. There was this summer my dad went off to Sunnyvale for a couple months because he used to work for Apple and I was all alone with my mum and my brother (it was really a quirky little time when my dad used to go on business trips—my mum was far less an authority figure even as she was more prone to strictness and she would always like her comfort, so we’d get to turn on the AC every evening and read books while she sewed and later played Candy Crush) and then when he came back he got a bunch of Hershey’s memorabilia and a few of these Silicon Valley books he used to read like the Walter Isaacson Steve Jobs biography. There was this other one which offhandedly mentioned Turing Machines and Fermat’s Last Theorem in a footnote and boy was I hooked. It was such a coincidence, too, coming a few days after the very same friend of mine who’d be writing SQL later had gotten me into some of these YouTube videos that purported to prove that \(1 + 2 + 3 + … = -1/12\) and other fallacious proofs; later I’d make a few myself of which one was about the Navier-Stokes problem. This was in, like, eighth grade. I did the Olympiad, too, of course I did the Olympiad. How could I not? I later learned all about how it wasn’t the old Romanian mathematical competition it used to be but basically a Jane Street funneling pipeline, how Jim Simons wasn’t the epitome of how mathematics is actually useful and the answer to the age-old question of but when am I going to use this in real life but, like, a hedge fund manager; I found out about Danny Lewin and Akamai, about the MAA and all these early attempts by Buzzard and these guys to automate and formalize the IMO. It was a high-school math contest is what it was, just a contest. It was supposed to be for kids.

Sure there was Turing, but there were many more: there was Andrew Wiles, for one, and Grigori Perelman, and David Hilbert and later Grothendieck and Weil and Galois and Gödel and God knows who else. It was so magical in the early days—I remember taking my first Theory of Computation class and being so excited at the prospect of studying Turing Machines that I skipped over the automata section and ended up doing quite poorly in it; my complexity theory course later had to make up for it, but even there a misplaced Chernoff bound left me just shy of an A+. Or reading about, like, zero-knowledge. It kind of blows your mind. This was the time in my life I had the most friends, and they were all kind of like me, which is saying something—we were nerds but there was something really pretty about the whole thing, doing math problems and then eating at KFC and watching Woody Allen movies and reading Hemingway after. My dad was always like math is always going to be around, you’ll never go wrong with math. When I started doing cryptography I was so excited by the prospects of cryptography. By then the internet had already started imprinting its Baudrillardian influence on the world and we’d been living in it for quite a while—college for me was a post-COVID thing. It’s a bit trivial to say but it’s hard to imagine how big of a deal COVID was. It was like the flip of a switch—there used to be the real world and there used to be the online world, and now all of a sudden they were the same thing. It was a big puncture in that ‘nothing ever happens’ future. Something really did happen that one time. Then when we came out of it things were kind of weird. There was some kind of bug in the air, like the air was less clean or something. Really it might have been about the little things, like how if you asked someone to meet you at 5PM they’d always be there before it but then after COVID you’d get a text at 4:45 saying sorry they can’t make it because they were tired. I guess by the time I’d started doing cryptography the old dream was already in the process of dying—there was the dream of the 80’s and 90’s of RSA and Elliptic Curves, then there were Worms and ILOVEYOU and Ransomware and Spectre, and then in the 2020s I’d been living the MPC everything dream and Apple was, like, implementing PSI and FHE and stuff—and they still are, as far as I can tell! They’ve got all these TEEs now that until a couple months ago I was worrying about—how are they going to post-quantum those TEEs? I’m talking about these things like they aren’t still a problem or anything; they still haven’t post-quantumed those damn TEEs. They’ll probably still have to, who knows—if they really need to of course I’ll stick around to do it.

The problem is that once you start checking out these things look so quaint. I decided today that I should write a diary, so here it goes. I woke up and saw that some internal model proved \(\mathbf{VP}\neq\mathbf{VNP}\). I went through the proof; it’s some kind of new geometric lifting technique followed by nonuniform diagonalization. Then I spent a little bit of time playing around with it and found a remarkable little proof of the size hierarchy theorem that bypasses the Shannon argument entirely and uses diagonalization. Isn’t that crazy? A year ago I’d be stoked, but now in this ‘endless feast’ of results it feels so pointless. A couple days ago they solved the Riemann Hypothesis. I guess in retrospect it was never supposed to be solved by human hands in the first place—wasn’t that what Leibniz and Hilbert and all those others were on about? But, you know, I can’t help but think that this wasn’t supposed to happen when I was supposed to witness it. I was supposed to die at 80 in a nice bed without ever knowing what the answer to Riemann was, and then someone would ask me and I’d say “Oh, I’d say we’re about fifty years off. We’ve got to do more Langlands, first. Remember the rising sea… there’s two ways to crack a walnut!” Stupid, but accurate. And \(\mathbf{P}\) vs. \(\mathbf{NP}\)? Pfft, cut me a break. We would still be stuck at \(\mathbf{NEXP}\not\subseteq\mathbf{P}/\mathbf{poly}\)…

I remember that one of the reasons I wanted to be a cryptographer was because of how cool it would be to tell someone that I was a person who broke codes for a living, a short-lived ambition because of all those cryptobros permeating into the mainstream; for a while I had to tell people that no I worked in cryptography and not cryptocurrency for fear of guys like Sam Bankman-Fried and ugly monkeys coming into their brain. The only time I ever told someone I broke codes for a living (I don’t) was this girl a couple years ago who thought it was really cool and wanted to know if I worked for the CIA, too, because I had a flip phone on me (really a banal reason, it’s because I was kind of addicted to doomscrolling); later I kissed her under the moonlight. I guess I should have kissed her harder, maybe she’d still be around, who knows. I was never a code-breaker. I wasn’t cool enough to be a cryptanalyst, I’m just a run-of-the-mill MPC and provable security guy. But it turns out no one was a code-breaker, not really, and now that they’re finally breaking all the codes it turns out they were all dead in the water anyway. I spent the last few months in Sunnyvale, harking back to that old summer of my dad’s, finally making my way to SF myself—‘go west, young man,’ or whatever they say about California, read a deluge of Jack Kerouac, too, went hiking through a bunch of redwood groves, ate ice cream with this girl in Berkeley. I also interned, I guess, doing something about threshold signatures from LWE, then I had this meeting yesterday about our project and we’re not sure what to make of it because word on the street’s that there’s a quasi-polynomial algorithm for MLWE now. Quantum algorithm, sure, but it’s not like that’s better. Another one’s saying they’ve got polytime for factoring. Can you believe it? They used to tell this story to us when we were undergrads about what would happen if someone solves factoring—they’d be king of the world, do all sorts of crimes. Cryptography would just be a glass wall to such a person. That’s the kind of thing we’re dealing with now. We finished the meeting alright, but there’s no consensus on what to do about the project—just a simple ‘let’s see what happens, until then business as usual.’ Business as usual! What a strange phrase. I don’t know what to make of it. I tried to do some business as usual—went to work today, started talking with my friends. Coffee-table conversation is about what jobs we’ll be able to do with Math PhDs after a year—I’m thinking I could do bartender; they thought I was too skinny for a lumberjack. Another one’s saying farmer. It’d be nice being a farmer, I guess—my grandparents were farmers. One of my friends is pregnant; she’s more scared than most, both her and her husband are cryptography PhDs, so if it’s hard for me to see these problems get struck down one by one you can imagine what it’s like to be them.

There’s a kind of unreality in the air about it. Yesterday I went on a walk with my neighbor—she’s a biologist, and she’s saying she’s hearing something about a wet lab at Anthropic, too, and there are all these job postings about turning biology into a verifiable domain. A few days ago I was at my friend’s thanksgiving party—went all the way to Saint-Denis for it, as well—and there’s this Irish girl there telling me about how her Masters’ is going so poorly because you press a button and the computer can do computational neuroscience now. My friend’s a math PhD. He just had the best year of his life—finally got a position, then he met his girlfriend, now he’s gained 10 pounds and was talking about how nothing makes sense while chugging beer and wondering how he’s going ever to tell his advisor that he pressed two buttons the other day and the answer to the biggest question in infinity categories popped out. Another one, this one from college, was at Berkeley for a Simons’ seminar. He says he thinks \(\mathbf{P}\) vs \(\mathbf{PSPACE}\) is close, it’s only a matter of time; now he’s applying to hedge funds to make a little bit of cash before they inevitably dry out, too. There’s a third friend in Boston who spent the summer in Tokyo solving his career-making problem, except that wouldn’t make anything close to a career in 2026 so instead he just ended up falling in love with a waitress and all he cares about is making his way back to her now. And a fourth one today asked me if I want to write a paper for Asiacrypt next year, which is going to be in Japan. That’s like, December next year, dude. Are we going to have conferences by then?

It sounds like I’m catastrophizing, and ideally I’d like not to, but on the other hand maybe that’s just the general mood of what it’s been like since COVID. See the thing about COVID is that in a lot of ways nobody ever recovered from it. There was a recession, but recessions are supposed to end; this one didn’t, not really, and if you look it up there are all these graphs about ‘K-shaped economy’ and how the entire GDP of the United States was being carried by none other than Artificial Intelligence which of course famously consists of lean companies that pay a few people a very large amount of money, while everyone else was doing these awkward data labeling jobs. All my friends got jobs—they are smart people, very intelligent, and the one time I tried, I did, too, but then we’re hardly entry-level grads with a simple degree; there are others I know with Masters’ in perfectly reasonable things like international finance who are waitressing because no one seems to be hiring in anything other than these speculative tech businesses who are trying to automate away the very people they’re hiring, anyway. That’s not how it’s supposed to go—there’s supposed to be some kind of bedrock to the economy that props up people to the middle class, we’re supposed to be upwardly mobile, if you don’t make it it’s because you’re lazy, remember? Whatever happened to that? Whatever happened to the millennial dream of starting your stupid expensive burger joint that played the Lumineers, wasn’t that going to be a thing, or is that just something people in Portland do now? SAG’s saying that they don’t want AI, but it’s not like Netflix cares, they’re in the money business, not in the TV show business—David Fincher is saying that it’s time to adapt and start de-aging actors using AI, something Disney’s been doing for like five years now with Luke Skywalker and Princess Leia. We used to have Flash Gordon, then we got Star Wars, now we’ve got, what—still Star Wars? Ostensibly these things make a lot of money, I can open up Wikipedia and see that No Way Home made two billion dollars, but no one seems to talk about it, I don’t know a single person who’s watched it. And I’m not some snob of cinema, I watched Endgame, I liked it, I cheered. I watched it in theaters like Day 3 or something. But there was the Odyssey, and I know a lot of people actually did watch that one, and then it got forgotten under this whole bit about Emily Watson commenting on how she disliked it, my friend’s girlfriend even wrote a whole op-ed about it on this website. That’s all people seem to talk about these days—op-eds and AI, that’s basically it. Oh yeah, and also Lindsey Clancy. We used to package these emotions into little boxes and contain them to daytime television so that the rest of us could go about our day having safely released them during a little 30-minute timespan. But now it’s like everyone’s on Jerry Springer all the time. Whatever happened to television?

And, you know, the thing about it is that everyone knows it. You know what else everyone talks about apart from those stupid op-eds and AI? How these op-eds are out to psyop you and how you shouldn’t read them. Everyone’s always trying to quit social media but never quite manages it. Does anyone actually like Twitter? I remember when Buzzfeed used to be a thing. We used to send each other quizzes like ‘what kind of potato are you?’ and nobody would say it’s cringe or get the ick or anything, they’d just giggle and reply. They thought it was so cute, Carly Rae Jepsen even put it in a song. Remember when Emotion came out? I listened to that thing back to front, it was such a thing. Or when The Life of Pablo dropped. I spent late October back where I came from, in St Louis, Missouri, after all these years—went to watch the first Blues game in the Enterprise Center I’d seen since 2007, was against the Habs, too, and though it ended like 2-0 the thing that got me wasn’t even how boring the game was or how it’s like $20 for a hot dog in those stands but that they play Seven Nation Army and Katy Perry’s Firework. They used to play Katy Perry’s Firework in 2010, the moment it came out. I didn’t even hear a bit of Blinding Lights, let alone Choosing Texas, but I heard the Chainsmokers’ Closer though. I had a good time in St Louis. Of course, my friend’s all cooked now, math PhD in WashU and all, but we played darts and bought some square pizza and watched Seinfeld. Later I went to New York City and visited the Seinfeld restaurant on 112th and Broadway, but it’s funny how people were doing that in both 1996 and 2026, there’s no difference. I was going to go to the Jimmy John’s downtown but ended up going to the diner right next to it which had a 4-dollar slinger and a 3-dollar chili mac, glad to see that there’s at least one place on Earth which is still cooking the right way—although the streets are all empty now, the place is so dead. It’s a city made for a million with a population of 300,000, and it’s just going down. Half the skyscrapers have no one in them. I talked to a friend about it and he said there’s no job, there’s no industry; everyone in Michigan is just moving to Detroit or Grand Rapids, who is hiring? They don’t manufacture in America anymore, they outsource it to countries where you can get the same work done by slaves for no money. But hey, they got some jazz—there was a new jazz club in town which had some real good music and some real good beer, and my friend took a great picture of me which when I later put up on Instagram was marked as ‘AI’ because my android phone has some kind of AI-assisted post-processing that smoothens out the acne. God knows how I actually look.

In some sense being from St Louis makes sense for me, just like being a cryptographer does—it’s neither here nor there, either East nor West, neither Midwestern nor Southern, and it peaked all the way back in 1904 when being next to a river actually meant something. The Mississippi passes through it. They used to teach us that when we were kids, how important the Mississippi was, the Louisiana Purchase. I went to the Gateway Arch Museum and they’re still feeding the kids that fiction about Pilgrims and Indians, they don’t mention the Trail of Tears even once. But the thing is you have to maintain the myths if you want to be a country. Where are the myths now? I don’t see them, all I see are people debunking them. Everyone knows the Nessie picture is an elephant’s trunk, not a single person thinks it’s the Loch Ness monster. Everyone’s seen the Zapruder film. And I guess everyone knows about the Trail of Tears, too—I’ve always thought that that’s what AI is, the ultimate reckoning, America’s comeuppance; the Europeans got paid back for colonialism through WWII, but why was America allowed to escape unscathed for what they did to the Indians? Now they’re going to get it back in kind. But that’s the exact kind of sentiment that won’t fly in this world, where the moment you say something even vaguely factually unsavory it gets hurled back at you with an accusation of lying. That’s the thing about these jobs. Everyone knows that something’s wrong. Everyone knows the economy isn’t working. Everyone can see that something terrible is going to happen, and it’s going to happen very soon, that there’s something deeply strange and unreal about the fact that you can go online and open up a website where 10,000 ‘agents’ are attacking the \(\mathbf{P}\) vs. \(\mathbf{NP}\) problem right now, live, and you can add more agents if you put down a few bucks of donations—‘the proof of \(\mathbf{P}\) vs. \(\mathbf{NP}\), sponsored by the Depend Adult Undergarment’—and I myself was indulging in this fiction of ‘oh this is just how the world is, that’s just how things are, nothing ever happens until two weeks ago’—

“When the policies of perestroika and glasnost’ (openness, public discussion) were introduced in 1985, most people did not anticipate that any radical changes would follow. These campaigns were thought to be no different from the endless state-orchestrated campaigns before them: campaigns that came and went, while life went on as usual. However, within a year or two the realization that something unimaginable was taking place began to dawn on the Soviet people. Many speak of having experienced a sudden “break of consciousness” (perelom soznania) and “stunning shock” (sil’neishii shok) quickly followed by excitement and readiness to participate in the transformation. Although different people experienced that moment differently, the type of experience they describe is similar, and many remember it vividly. […] Tonya, a school teacher born in Leningrad in 1966, describes the moment she first realized, around 1987, that “something impossible” (chto-to nevozmozhnoe) was taking place: “I was reading on the metro and suddenly experienced an utter shock. I remember that moment very well.… I was reading Lev Razgon’s story ‘Uninvented’ (Nepridumannoe), just published in Iunost’ [the literary journal Youth]. I could never have imagined that anything even remotely comparable would be published. After that the stream[…]”

From Alexai Yurchak, Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More. Didn’t that happen to the Jacobian Conjecture earlier this year? Then Navier-Stokes? But I didn’t think I believed it, not really, until Riemann fell. It takes a long time to realize how bitter the bitter lesson is.

But life goes on anyway. Isn’t that weird? A computer solved the Riemann Hypothesis, but life goes on anyway. And now, finally, something has happened. Time is no longer at an end. There is no slow decline. There is no looking back upon the California of the older days, the California ‘where it is easy to Dial-A-Devotion, but hard to buy a book’ à la Didion, is well and truly dead. ‘Up Carnelian Avenue from the Kapu Kai, which means “Forbidden Seas,” the subdivision flags whip in the harsh wind. “HALF-ACRE RANCHES! SNACK BARS! TRAVERTINE ENTRIES! $95 DOWN.”’ I was there, I saw it. That’s not what the signs say anymore. Now they say SUPERINTELLIGENCE.

I remember reading Didion as a kid. I first came across her while writing college essays—I wanted to look something up as a reference, because I’d read all these gee-whiz essays that successful applicants to the University of Chicago had written about things like whether ‘a hot dog is a sandwich,’ all these things which back before COVID felt quite mundane when there was still the fiction that getting into a good university was largely a function of your SATs but would qualify as psychological torture now. I wanted to write something different, a real essay, you know, so I popped open my computer and spent a very long time reading about some of the best essays that had been written in human history—I read Cicero’s Catilinarian orations but it was clear that they were hardly appropriate for this sort of thing, and so I found myself skimming through Joan Didion’s Goodbye to All That not knowing how it had served for nearly forty years at that point as the template for mid-twenties female malaise, how it had practically minted Didion as the poster child for that particular kind of countercultural cigarette-dread (of course I thought she was hot, too, but then I was a teenage boy with literary pretensions). All I thought was that I wanted to write the way she wrote. I still maintained then this fiction of wanting to be a writer, like a real writer, that is, someone who wrote short stories for The New Yorker, someone who would mail in a manuscript and they’d look at it and slap it down at the editor’s desk with a cigar and the proclamation that ‘there’s something here, alright, it’s the next A Perfect Day for Bananafish.’ Recently I’ve gotten some buzz from those very same upscale NYC places, too, but it seems like what’s the point. There’s too much writing. And who reads anymore, anyway, who’s going to order The New Yorker or another one of those intellectual magazines—this used to be a country in which Woody Allen could write a short story about a fictional character coming to life and win the O Henry Prize, hell this used to be a country where Donna Tartt could write The Goldfinch and it’d win a pulitzer, controversial as it was, what’ve we got now, R.F. Kuang?

I was in New York a couple weeks ago. Speaking at a conference, in fact, a cryptography conference, and what I wanted to do was take a camera and record a bunch of videos of all the young cryptographers talking about what drew them to mathematics in the first place and their opinions on what the future of mathematics would be like. But what I found there was so sickeningly normal that any desire I had for such an endeavor evaporated immediately. Just a bunch of oldheads talking about how this technology is the new evolution of computer science and how it’s our turn to adapt. Can’t they look around? Can’t they see they’re trying to stuff the industrial revolution into the first quarter of the Super Bowl—now’s not the time to be asking about lower bounds on threshold signatures, it’s the time to be asking how many agents you can stuff on the head of a pin. ‘We should embrace AI, it makes us better cryptographers.’ You think there’s going to be a cryptography when this is all said and done? Pack it up, the game’s over. You got your 15 minutes, hell, you got your 50 years—they’ve finally done it. They’ve created a machine that thinks. And so instead of spending time at NYU I decided to spend some time in Central Park, instead. I walked around, thinking of Didion. This New York wasn’t her New York, that’s for sure—hers had dirt and grime and Robert de Niro in it, but there was still something about the place that made it feel like people actually lived here. It’s strange how few places feel like they actually have people in them. People leave signs—in New York they leave trash on the subway. And there I was, at the center of the world. I thought of what would have happened if you’d told me about this 10 years ago as a teenager. ‘You’re going to be in New York City, you’re going to be 23 years old, you’re going to be giving a talk to a large audience of experts and they’re all going to be clamoring to every word and later they’ll come and ask you to drinks coffee and elaborate, you’re going to walk around Central Park in the fall, you’re going to play poker with a hedge fund manager and a Google cryptographer while drinking horchatas on the 9th floor of an Upper East Side Apartment, you’re going to walk around Greenwich Village with a friend from college and eat a nice slice of pizza and a chopped cheese sandwich, you’re going to be eating at a Chick-fil-A at 10PM with your oldest friend from high school and discussing about how AI is going to take over the world very very soon and later walk 100 blocks in the cold Manhattan fall while the Christmas lights are on and agonize about a girl who may or may not be waiting for you when you take the plane right back down to Paris on Sunday’—I would’ve said that’s best case scenario. Not bad for a kid from Missouri whose grandparents were farmers. Didn’t I make it, in the end? You can’t say I didn’t try.

But more than all the romance and the enchantment what I got, from New York, was simply a long stretch of deja vu. After San Francisco I knew the world was going to end and so I went to St Louis, to Chicago and to New York. I knew what St Louis was like. I knew what Chicago was like, too. I got into a funny situation with a date with a receptionist and a waiter, and it was all like an Edward Hopper painting, but it was a lot less than New York. The thing about New York was that all I felt was like I’d already been there before. But how could that be? I’d never seen the Chrysler building; maybe once when I was three. But it was because of all these books and movies, of all these pictures and all these photos and all the talk and all the discourse—of course I’d already been there. Was there anything new? You can go anywhere now, just press a button and you’re there. You can know anything. You want to know if \(\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}\)? Well there you go. Press a button, and you’ll know. You want to know where you saw that stupid little quote that was stuck in your head? Just ask! That’s the problem with genies. You get what you want, and then it doesn’t matter. But it was inevitable that we’d make this, in the end. That’s what we are—information fiends. I’ve been to New York. I’ve been to Paris, I’ve been to London, I’ve been to Vancouver, I’ve been to Tokyo. Yeah, the Japanese eat KFC for Christmas, did you know that? Everyone knows that. Everyone knows everything. I can ask a question to the fucking machine and it’ll tell me everything I need to know about someone. I can read every book, I can watch every movie. People talk about abundance—don’t you have enough? Isn’t this abundance? You can talk to anyone in the world, whenever you want. You don’t have to wait for anything! Isn’t this the world we created? There used to be a world, a real world, a world that was limited—a world which had resources. I’m not the first one in my family to go to New York City, my dad went there, once. They flew him out cause he knew how Adobe Reader worked. Was a game-changer back in the day. Spent six months going to a video store and watching every Hong Kong movie in existence. You ask him a question and he’ll be like ‘Oh yeah, that’s from Drunken Master II.’ But that’s all him. I can tell you the same thing, but it’s not me, it’s the voice of the shared human consciousness speaking through me. I’ve seen Drunken Master II, too, but it doesn’t mean anything to me. To me it’s a 1994 Jackie Chan film directed by Lau Kar-leung, to him it’s a Chinatown apartment in an unfamiliar world. We’ve got too much stuff. We’ve got too many papers, we’ve got too much money, too much math. It’s all more more more. Now people want to live forever. Fuck it, I want to live forever. You really think it’s possible to live like this? To live forever? Isn’t what you’ve got enough? And they’ve taken all our stuff, all of it, not a joke, every single thing every written—and they’ve used it to summon the devil and they’ve locked him inside a datacenter and he’s going to give us everything we ever wanted, ABUNDANCE, everything—all the numbers are going to hit infinity.

But there’s no need to worry—things always work out in the end. We had the Great War, it ended. It’s all fine, now. We got our 80 years. We had a great run—we did books, movies, the judiciary system, contraception, the space race, pepsi-cola, communism, we did all of it. It was good stuff. Broke apart by the end, we couldn’t help ourselves. But now the great Ouroboros of technology is here and it’s eating itself. Something has finally happened.
